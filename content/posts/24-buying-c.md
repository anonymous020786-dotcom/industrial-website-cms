Every maintenance manager faces the same balancing act with spare parts. Hold too few, and a minor failure becomes a long shutdown while parts are sourced. Hold too many, and capital is tied up in parts that may never be used — or that become obsolete. A structured approach helps you stock the right parts in the right quantities.

## Step 1: List your equipment and rank criticality

Start with the equipment, not the parts. Rank each machine or system by:

- impact of failure on production and safety;
- availability of backup equipment;
- cost of downtime per hour or day.

Focus detailed spares planning on the most critical equipment first.

## Step 2: Identify critical components

For each critical machine, identify components whose failure would stop it. For cranes and hydraulic systems, typical candidates include:

- hydraulic cylinders, pumps and motors;
- valves and solenoid coils;
- crane wheels, brakes and couplings;
- gearboxes and motors;
- control and safety components;
- wear parts such as seals, filters and brake linings.

## Step 3: Assess lead time and availability

For each component, determine:

- typical lead time from suppliers;
- whether it is standard or custom;
- whether it is obsolete or at risk of obsolescence;
- whether it can be repaired quickly instead of replaced.

## Step 4: Use a simple risk matrix

| | Short lead time | Long lead time |
|---|---|---|
| **High criticality** | Hold wear items; consider spares | **Hold spares** |
| **Low criticality** | Buy when needed | Consider spares or repair plans |

Components that are critical **and** slow to obtain are the strongest candidates for stock.

## Step 5: Set stock levels

- **Wear parts:** base quantities on consumption history and reorder points.
- **Insurance spares:** usually one per group of identical machines.
- **Repairable spares:** consider exchange units — fit the spare immediately and repair the failed part for the shelf.

## Step 6: Standardise where possible

Standardising components across machines — seal sizes, valve types, pumps and fasteners — reduces the number of different spares needed. When replacing equipment, consider standardisation opportunities.

## Step 7: Address obsolescence proactively

Older equipment often has parts that are no longer available. Identify these early and:

- reverse-engineer components and keep approved drawings;
- order spares before failures occur;
- consider upgrades that replace obsolete subsystems.

## Step 8: Store and maintain spares properly

Spares that deteriorate on the shelf are useless when needed:

- protect machined surfaces from corrosion;
- store seals and rubber parts away from heat, light and ozone;
- store cylinders horizontally with ports capped and rods protected;
- rotate shaft-mounted spares periodically where recommended;
- label clearly with equipment and location.

## Step 9: Review regularly

Review the spares list after breakdowns and at least annually. Add parts that caused unexpected downtime, reduce stock of items that never move, and remove parts for retired equipment.

## Working with suppliers

Manufacturers can support your strategy by keeping drawings on file, holding customer-specific stock, offering exchange programmes and committing to priority lead times for breakdowns.

When a part is obsolete or drawings are missing, reverse engineering can recreate it. The quality and speed of that process depend heavily on what you send to the manufacturer. A well-prepared sample with good supporting information can save days of back-and-forth.

## What to send

### The part itself

The actual worn or failed part is the most valuable reference. Where possible, send the complete component rather than fragments. If the part is broken, send all pieces.

### Mating parts or their dimensions

A part's dimensions often depend on what it fits into or onto. For example:

- a pin's diameter relates to the bushes or holes it fits;
- a wheel's bore relates to the axle or bearing;
- a gear relates to its mating gear and shaft.

Send mating parts if practical, or measure and record their dimensions.

### An unworn example if available

If you have an unworn or less worn identical part — on another machine or in stock — it can show original dimensions more accurately.

## Information to include

| Information | Why it helps |
|---|---|
| Machine make, model and serial number | Context and possible original data |
| Location in the machine | Function and loading |
| Quantity needed now and in future | Planning and cost |
| How the part failed | Opportunities to improve |
| Operating conditions | Load, speed, temperature, environment |
| Photos of the part installed | Orientation and interfaces |
| Required documentation | Inspection reports, certificates |

## Preparing the part

1. **Do not clean aggressively.** Remove loose dirt and oil, but avoid grinding, wire brushing or polishing that changes dimensions or removes evidence of wear patterns.
2. **Do not straighten or repair** bent or damaged parts. The damage itself may reveal useful information.
3. **Label the part** with your company name, contact person and reference.
4. **Mark orientation** if relevant (for example, which side faced outward).
5. **Protect machined surfaces** with a light oil or wrap to prevent corrosion in transit.

## Packaging and shipping

- Use sturdy packaging suitable for the weight.
- Protect fragile features such as threads, gear teeth and sealing surfaces.
- Include a copy of your information sheet inside the package.
- Send photographs and information by email in advance so engineering review can start before the part arrives.

## When you cannot send the part

If the part cannot be removed or must remain in service, a manufacturer may be able to measure it on site, or work from detailed photos and measurements. Complex parts or parts with critical fits are best measured directly.

## What happens next

The manufacturer will typically:

1. inspect and measure the part;
2. assess material and hardness;
3. produce a drawing for your approval;
4. manufacture and inspect the replacement;
5. return the original sample if requested.

## Keep the drawing

Once approved, keep the drawing in your records. Future orders become simple and fast, and you are no longer dependent on a single supplier's knowledge.

Hydraulic systems make heavy work look easy. That same power makes them dangerous when something goes wrong or when maintenance is carried out unsafely. Many hydraulic injuries happen because people assume a stopped machine is safe. This guide covers the essential safety principles. It is not a substitute for your site's procedures, the equipment manufacturer's instructions or appropriate training.

## Understand stored energy

A hydraulic system can hold energy long after the pump stops:

- **Trapped pressure** in cylinders, hoses and valves held closed by check valves.
- **Accumulators**, which store pressurised oil specifically to release it later.
- **Suspended loads** held up by cylinders, which can fall if pressure is released.
- **Raised structures** such as booms, tipper bodies and press rams.
- **Hot oil and components**, which can cause burns.

Before any maintenance, all of these energy sources must be identified and controlled.

## Isolation and lock-out

Follow your site's lock-out/tag-out procedures:

1. Stop the machine and isolate power sources.
2. **Lower loads to the ground** or **support them mechanically** with suitable blocks, props or stands.
3. **Relieve system pressure** using the correct procedure.
4. **Discharge accumulators** and verify with a gauge.
5. Lock and tag isolation points.
6. **Verify zero energy** before starting work.

## Hydraulic injection injuries

High-pressure oil escaping from a pinhole leak can penetrate skin. These injuries may look minor at first but are medical emergencies requiring immediate specialist treatment.

- **Never use your hand to search for leaks.** Use cardboard or wood, and wear appropriate protection.
- Seek urgent medical attention for any suspected injection injury, and tell medical staff it involved hydraulic fluid.

## Hose safety

Hoses fail through age, abrasion, kinking, incorrect assembly and over-pressurisation. A bursting hose can whip violently and spray hot oil.

- Inspect hoses regularly and replace damaged or aged hoses.
- Use hoses, fittings and assemblies rated for the system pressure.
- Use hose restraints and guards where failure could injure people.
- Route hoses to avoid abrasion, heat and pinch points.

## Never work under unsupported loads

Hydraulic cylinders and valves can leak. A load held only by hydraulics can drift down or drop suddenly if a hose fails. Always use mechanical supports rated for the load before working under raised equipment.

## Correct tools and procedures

- Never loosen fittings under pressure.
- Use calibrated gauges when testing.
- Bleed air carefully.
- Use the right tools for disassembly — cylinders may contain trapped pressure or spring forces.
- Follow manufacturer procedures for adjusting relief and load-holding valves; incorrect settings can create dangerous conditions.

## Personal protective equipment

Depending on the task: safety glasses or face shields, gloves, protective clothing and safety footwear. Hot oil and high-pressure sprays are real hazards.

## Housekeeping and fire risk

Clean up oil spills promptly to prevent slips. Hydraulic oil leaking onto hot surfaces can ignite; fix leaks and keep ignition sources away from hydraulic equipment.

## Training and competence

Hydraulic work should be carried out by trained, competent people. Operators should be trained to recognise warning signs — leaks, drift, unusual noise — and report them.

## Quick safety checklist

| Before work | Done? |
|---|---|
| Loads lowered or mechanically supported | ☐ |
| Power isolated and locked out | ☐ |
| Pressure relieved and verified | ☐ |
| Accumulators discharged and verified | ☐ |
| PPE in place | ☐ |
| Correct tools and procedures available | ☐ |
