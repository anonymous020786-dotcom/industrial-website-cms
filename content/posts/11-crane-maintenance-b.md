On any crane, the brakes stand between a controlled lift and a dropped load. Hoist brakes must hold the full rated load securely, and travel brakes must stop the crane and trolley smoothly without skidding or shock. Regular brake maintenance is essential for both safety and reliability.

## Types of crane brakes

### Electromagnetic disc brakes

Common on modern hoist and travel motors. A spring applies the brake; an electromagnet releases it when power is applied. If power fails, the brake applies automatically — a fail-safe design.

### Electro-hydraulic thruster drum brakes

Found on many larger industrial cranes. Springs apply brake shoes to a drum, and an electro-hydraulic thruster lifts them off when energised.

### Mechanical load brakes

Some hoists include a mechanical load brake in the gearbox that controls lowering speed, in addition to the motor brake.

## Common brake problems

| Problem | Possible causes |
|---|---|
| Load drifts or slips | Worn linings, incorrect air gap, contamination by oil or grease |
| Brake does not release fully | Coil fault, rectifier failure, thruster failure, incorrect adjustment |
| Overheating | Brake dragging, excessive inching, incorrect adjustment |
| Long stopping distance | Worn linings, weak springs, glazed surfaces |
| Noise or chatter | Uneven wear, loose components, damaged disc or drum |
| Delayed application | Control circuit problems, rectifier issues |

## Inspection and maintenance routine

**Always isolate the crane, secure loads and follow lock-out procedures before working on brakes.**

### Regular checks

- **Holding test:** during pre-use checks, confirm the hoist holds a load without creeping.
- **Stopping performance:** watch for increasing stopping distances on travel motions.
- **Noise and smell:** burning smells or squeals indicate dragging or overheating.

### Periodic maintenance

- **Measure lining wear** and replace before the minimum thickness is reached.
- **Check and adjust the air gap** on electromagnetic brakes to the manufacturer's specification. As linings wear, the air gap increases and the brake may fail to release properly or apply slowly.
- **Inspect discs and drums** for scoring, cracks, heat checking and glazing.
- **Check springs** for corrosion, cracks and correct tension.
- **Inspect pins and linkages** on drum brakes for wear and free movement.
- **Check thrusters** for oil level and leaks.
- **Test electrical components** such as coils, rectifiers and connections.
- **Keep brakes clean and dry**, free from oil, grease and dust.

## Lining replacement

Replace linings in sets so braking is even. Use linings specified for the brake model and duty. After replacement, adjust air gaps and run in according to the manufacturer's procedure, then test holding and stopping performance.

## Why brakes fail early

- **Excessive inching** (frequent short movements) generates heat and wear.
- **Wrong adjustment** causes drag or poor holding.
- **Oil contamination** from leaking gearboxes reduces friction dramatically.
- **Overloading** the crane strains brakes beyond their design.

Addressing these causes extends brake life significantly.

## Brake testing after maintenance

After any brake work, test performance before returning the crane to service: confirm holding with a test load, check stopping distances and verify that the brake applies when power is removed. Major repairs or modifications may require examination by a competent person under local regulations.

## Spare parts

Brake linings, coils, rectifiers and springs are worth keeping in stock for critical cranes. Where original parts are no longer available, brake components such as drums, discs and linkages can often be manufactured to drawing or sample.

A 10-tonne crane in a maintenance workshop and a 10-tonne crane in a steel mill have the same rated capacity, but they live very different lives. The workshop crane might lift its full load a few times a week. The steel mill crane may lift heavy loads continuously, around the clock. Their components must be designed differently — and that is what duty classification describes.

## What is crane duty classification?

Duty classification groups cranes and their mechanisms according to **how hard they work**. Several standards systems are used internationally, including **ISO 4301**, **FEM** rules widely used in Europe, and **CMAA** specifications in North America. Each system uses its own class designations, but they consider similar factors.

## The two main factors

### 1. Load spectrum

How often does the crane lift loads close to its rated capacity? A crane that regularly lifts near-maximum loads has a heavier load spectrum than one that mostly lifts light loads.

### 2. Usage (class of utilisation)

How many working cycles or operating hours will the crane accumulate over its design life? More cycles mean more fatigue on structures and more wear on mechanisms.

Combining load spectrum and usage gives a duty class for the crane as a whole and for individual mechanisms such as the hoist, trolley travel and long travel.

## Why it matters for components

Components on higher-duty cranes need:

- **larger safety factors** against fatigue;
- **larger or harder wheels** to handle repeated rolling loads;
- **larger sheave and drum diameters** relative to rope size to reduce rope fatigue;
- **heavier-duty gearboxes, bearings and brakes**;
- **motors rated** for higher starts per hour and duty.

A part that performs well on a light-duty crane may wear out rapidly on a heavy-duty crane.

## Typical applications by duty level

| Duty level | Typical applications |
|---|---|
| Light | Maintenance workshops, powerhouses, standby cranes |
| Moderate | General manufacturing, assembly, warehouses |
| Heavy | Fabrication shops, steel stockyards, busy production lines |
| Severe | Steel mills, foundries, container ports, continuous process plants |

## When duty class changes over time

Many cranes are installed for one duty and later used for another. Production increases, shift patterns change and loads become heavier. Signs that a crane is working beyond its original duty include:

- rapidly wearing wheels, rope or brakes;
- overheating motors and gearboxes;
- fatigue cracks in structures;
- frequent breakdowns despite good maintenance.

If this happens, review the crane's duty against its current use. Upgrading components — or the crane — may be necessary.

## Ordering replacement parts

When ordering replacement wheels, sheaves, drums, pins or gear components, tell your supplier:

- the crane capacity and span;
- the duty class or a description of how the crane is used;
- operating hours or cycles per day;
- environmental conditions such as heat or dust;
- any history of premature wear.

This information allows the manufacturer to select appropriate materials, heat treatment and dimensions — and possibly suggest improvements, such as harder wheel treads for heavy-duty cranes.

## Duty classification and compliance

Duty classification is part of crane design and is defined by the crane manufacturer and applicable standards. Changes to a crane's rated capacity, duty or structure should be assessed by qualified engineers and may require re-certification under local regulations.

Cranes often outlive the companies that built them. Twenty or thirty years after installation, a failed wheel, coupling or gear may have no available replacement. Drawings may be lost and the original manufacturer may no longer exist. Reverse engineering offers a reliable way to recreate the part.

## What is reverse engineering?

Reverse engineering is the process of measuring and analysing an existing part to create a drawing and specification, then manufacturing a replacement. For crane parts, it typically applies to:

- wheels, axles and bearing housings;
- pins, bushes and shafts;
- sheaves and drums;
- couplings and gear components;
- brake drums and linkages;
- brackets and fabricated components.

## The reverse engineering process

### 1. Collect the part and information

The worn or failed part is the starting point. Information about the crane — capacity, duty, mating parts and failure history — is equally important, because the replacement must work in the real application.

### 2. Clean and inspect

The part is cleaned and inspected for wear patterns, cracks and damage. Wear patterns often reveal problems such as misalignment that should be corrected in the new design.

### 3. Measure

Dimensions are measured using calipers, micrometers, bore gauges and other tools. Where surfaces are worn, the original dimensions must be determined by measuring unworn areas, considering mating parts and applying engineering judgement.

### 4. Identify material and heat treatment

Hardness testing and, where needed, material analysis help determine the original material and heat treatment. This is particularly important for wheels, shafts and gears, where hardness affects wear life and strength.

### 5. Create the drawing

A detailed drawing is produced with dimensions, tolerances, material, heat treatment and surface finish requirements.

### 6. Customer approval

The customer reviews and approves the drawing before manufacture. This step ensures nothing important has been missed and gives the customer a drawing for future orders.

### 7. Manufacture and inspect

The part is manufactured and inspected against the drawing, with a dimensional and hardness report supplied.

## Opportunities to improve

Reverse engineering is not only about copying. It is an opportunity to:

- specify a **harder or tougher material** where wear was excessive;
- improve **heat treatment** for longer life;
- correct **design weaknesses** revealed by the failure;
- standardise dimensions to match **readily available bearings and seals**.

## How to get the best result

| Do | Avoid |
|---|---|
| Send the actual worn part if possible | Relying only on photos for complex parts |
| Provide mating part details | Sending parts without context |
| Describe how the part failed | Assuming the original design was ideal |
| Keep the approved drawing on file | Losing the drawing again |

## Plan before parts fail

The best time to reverse-engineer a critical part is **before** it fails. During planned maintenance, identify components with no available spares, measure them and create drawings. You can then order spares in advance and avoid emergency shutdowns.

## Safety and compliance

Load-bearing crane components must be designed and manufactured to appropriate engineering standards. Replacement parts should match or exceed the original specification, and significant changes should be reviewed by qualified engineers.
