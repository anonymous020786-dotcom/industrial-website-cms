When a hydraulic cylinder starts leaking or loses performance, maintenance teams face a familiar question: should we repair it or buy a new one? The right answer depends on the condition of the main components, the cost and lead time of each option, and — most importantly — why the cylinder failed in the first place.

## What a cylinder repair usually involves

A professional repair is more than replacing seals. A typical rebuild includes:

1. **Dismantling and cleaning** all components.
2. **Inspection** of the rod, barrel, piston, gland and mountings, with measurements against tolerances.
3. **Repairs** such as polishing, re-chroming or replacing the rod, honing the barrel, and replacing bushes or bearings.
4. **Resealing** with a complete, correctly specified seal kit.
5. **Assembly and pressure testing** to confirm there is no internal or external leakage.
6. **A findings report** explaining what failed and why.

## When repair makes sense

Repair is usually the better option when:

- the **barrel bore is within tolerance** or can be honed without exceeding it;
- the **rod is straight**, with damage limited to light scratches or chrome wear that can be polished or re-plated;
- the cylinder is a **custom or obsolete design** with a long lead time for a new unit;
- the mountings and welds are sound;
- the failure was caused by a correctable issue, such as worn seals or contamination.

Repair is often faster than ordering a new custom cylinder and keeps the original geometry, which avoids machine modifications.

## When replacement is the better option

Replacement is usually more sensible when:

- the **barrel is bulged, cracked or worn** beyond honing limits;
- the **rod is bent** or the base material is damaged under the chrome;
- welds or mounting points are cracked or fatigued;
- the cylinder has been repaired several times and keeps failing;
- the original design was **undersized for the application**;
- a standard cylinder is readily available at a lower cost than the repair.

## Comparing cost honestly

Look beyond the purchase price. Consider:

| Factor | Repair | Replacement |
|---|---|---|
| Direct cost | Usually lower for large or custom cylinders | Can be lower for small standard cylinders |
| Lead time | Often days | Days for stock items, weeks for custom units |
| Fit | Original geometry retained | May need modifications if not identical |
| Reliability | Good if root cause is fixed | Good, and a chance to improve the design |
| Documentation | Findings report | New drawings and test certificate |

The cost of downtime often outweighs the difference between the two options. If a crane or production line is stopped, the fastest reliable solution is usually the cheapest overall.

## Always find the root cause

Whichever option you choose, ask why the cylinder failed. Common causes include contamination, misalignment, rod damage, excessive heat and pressure spikes. Repairing or replacing a cylinder without fixing the cause simply resets the clock to the next failure.

A good repair report should tell you, for example, that the rod was scored by contamination, or that uneven bushing wear indicates side loading. That information lets you fix the system, not just the cylinder.

## Consider an upgrade

A failure is also an opportunity to improve. During repair or replacement you can:

- increase rod diameter to resist buckling;
- specify harder or thicker rod coatings for harsh environments;
- upgrade seals for higher temperatures or speeds;
- add cushioning to reduce shock;
- change mountings to spherical bearings to accommodate misalignment.

## Keep a spare for critical equipment

For cylinders whose failure would stop production, consider holding a reconditioned spare. When a cylinder fails, swap in the spare immediately and repair the failed unit without time pressure.

The most reliable way to decide is to have the cylinder inspected and measured. We provide honest repair-or-replace recommendations with a written findings report. Explore our [hydraulic cylinder services](/products/hydraulic-cylinders) or [request an inspection quote](/quote).

## Summary

- Repair when the barrel and rod are within limits and the design suits the application.
- Replace when major components are bent, cracked or worn beyond tolerance, or the design is inadequate.
- Factor in downtime, lead time and reliability, not just price.
- Always fix the root cause, and consider upgrades while the cylinder is apart.

Every time a hydraulic cylinder extends, its piston rod leaves the protection of the barrel and meets dust, moisture and impacts. When it retracts, the rod passes back through the wiper and rod seal. The condition of the rod surface therefore controls two things: **how long the seals last** and **how well the rod resists corrosion and damage**.

That is why most hydraulic piston rods are induction hardened and hard-chrome plated.

## What hard chrome plating does

Hard chrome is an electroplated layer of chromium applied to the polished rod. It provides:

- **High surface hardness**, resisting scratches and wear from seals and contaminants.
- **Corrosion resistance**, protecting the steel underneath from moisture and chemicals.
- **A smooth, low-friction surface** that allows seals to slide with minimal wear.
- **Good oil retention** characteristics when finished correctly, helping to lubricate the seal contact.

## Why induction hardening comes first

Chrome is hard but thin. If the steel underneath is soft, an impact can dent the base metal and crack the chrome above it. **Induction hardening** hardens the outer layer of the steel rod before plating, providing a strong foundation that supports the chrome and resists dents. The combination of a hardened case and hard chrome is standard for demanding mobile and industrial applications.

## Surface finish matters

The finish of the rod after plating and polishing is carefully controlled. Too rough, and the surface acts like a file on the seals. Too smooth, and it may not retain enough lubricating film, causing friction and stick-slip. Rod manufacturers and cylinder builders work to specified roughness ranges recommended by seal manufacturers.

## Common causes of chrome damage

| Damage | Typical cause |
|---|---|
| Scratches along the rod | Contamination, failed wiper, debris |
| Dents and chips | Impact from rocks, tools or handling |
| Pitting and rust spots | Moisture, salt, chemicals, long idle periods |
| Flaking chrome | Impact damage to an unhardened base, poor plating adhesion |
| Weld spatter burns | Welding nearby without protecting the rod |

## How rod damage destroys seals

A rod seal is designed to slide over a smooth surface. A single nick or pit drags across the seal lip thousands of times, cutting it and creating a leak path. Replacing the seal without repairing the rod will result in another leak, often within days. This is why rod inspection is part of every proper cylinder repair.

## Repair options for damaged rods

- **Polishing:** removes light surface marks when the chrome is intact.
- **Re-chroming:** the old chrome is removed, the rod is ground, re-plated and polished back to size. Suitable when the base material is undamaged.
- **New rod:** necessary when the rod is bent, deeply pitted, cracked, or the base metal is damaged.

## Protecting rods in service

1. **Retract rods when equipment is idle**, especially outdoors.
2. **Use rod boots or guards** in heavy-debris environments such as mining and demolition.
3. **Protect rods during welding** on or near the machine.
4. **Keep wipers in good condition** so dirt does not reach the seals.
5. **Clean exposed rods** on equipment that works in mud, cement or chemical environments.
6. **Use suitable rod coatings** where corrosion is severe.

## Alternatives for harsh environments

In very corrosive environments — marine, offshore, chemical plants — standard hard chrome may not be enough. Options include thicker or multi-layer chrome, nickel-chrome systems, stainless steel rods, and thermal spray or laser-clad coatings. These options cost more but can dramatically extend service life where corrosion is the main failure mode.

## The bottom line

The rod surface is one of the most important parts of a hydraulic cylinder. Specifying the right base material, hardening and plating, and protecting the rod in service, prevents the majority of rod seal leaks.

"The boom won't hold" or "the platform creeps down overnight" — cylinder drift is one of the most common hydraulic complaints. It is also one of the most frequently misdiagnosed. Many cylinders are resealed when the real problem is a valve, and some valves are replaced when the piston seal was at fault.

This guide explains what causes drift and how to diagnose it methodically and safely.

## What is cylinder drift?

Drift is unwanted movement of a cylinder when the control valve is in neutral and the cylinder should be holding position. It usually shows up as a load slowly lowering, or a cylinder slowly extending or retracting.

Drift happens when oil escapes from the chamber that is holding the load. That oil can escape through three main routes:

1. **Across the piston seal** inside the cylinder (internal leakage).
2. **Through the control valve or load-holding valve** back to tank.
3. **Externally**, through a leaking fitting, hose or rod seal.

## External leakage: the easy one

Start with the obvious. Inspect the cylinder, hoses and fittings for visible oil. A rod seal leak or a weeping fitting on the loaded side can cause drift. External leaks are also a safety and environmental hazard, so they should be fixed regardless.

## Internal piston seal leakage

If the piston seal is worn or damaged, oil passes from the high-pressure side to the low-pressure side of the piston. In a double-acting cylinder, this lets the load move as oil transfers between chambers.

Typical causes include worn or damaged piston seals, a scored barrel, a loose piston nut or a cracked piston.

## Valve leakage

Spool-type directional control valves are not leak-tight by design. A small clearance between the spool and the body allows some leakage, which increases as the valve wears. If the circuit relies on the spool to hold a load, some drift is expected over time.

This is why load-holding circuits use **pilot-operated check valves** or **counterbalance valves** mounted close to the cylinder. If these valves are contaminated, incorrectly adjusted or worn, they leak and allow drift.

## A safe, methodical diagnosis

**Safety first:** always support loads mechanically before working on a hydraulic circuit. Never disconnect hoses from a cylinder holding a load. Relieve pressure and follow your site's isolation procedures.

### Step 1: Observe the drift

Note how fast the load drifts, in which direction, and whether it changes with temperature. Drift that increases as oil gets hot often indicates internal leakage in valves or worn seals, because hot oil is thinner.

### Step 2: Check the load-holding valves

If the cylinder has counterbalance or pilot-operated check valves, check their settings and condition. Contamination on a valve seat is a common cause and may be cured by cleaning.

### Step 3: Isolate the cylinder from the valve

With the load safely supported or the cylinder unloaded, isolation tests can separate cylinder leakage from valve leakage. One common workshop method is to fully extend or retract the cylinder against its stop, then check whether oil flows from the opposite port when pressure is applied. A steady flow indicates oil passing the piston seal.

Because these tests involve pressurised components, they should only be performed by trained personnel following the equipment manufacturer's procedures.

### Step 4: Bench test if necessary

The most reliable test is on a test bench, where the cylinder can be pressurised at each end and leakage measured directly. This is standard during professional cylinder repair.

## Interpreting results

| Finding | Likely cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Oil flows past piston under pressure | Piston seal or barrel damage | Repair cylinder |
| Cylinder holds on bench, drifts on machine | Valve leakage | Service or replace valves |
| Drift only when hot | Worn seals or valves, oil viscosity | Inspect, check cooling |
| Visible external leak | Rod seal or fitting failure | Repair leak |

## Preventing drift

- Keep oil clean to protect seals and valve seats.
- Use proper load-holding valves on critical cylinders.
- Maintain oil temperature within the recommended range.
- Inspect and replace seals before they fail completely.
