Crane wheels and rails work as a system. The rail supports and guides the wheel; the wheel loads and wears the rail. When rails wear or move out of alignment, wheels wear faster — and when wheels are misaligned, rails suffer. Effective maintenance treats them together.

## How crane rails wear

### Head wear

The top of the rail head wears from rolling contact. Uneven head wear changes the contact between wheel and rail, concentrating stress and accelerating wheel tread wear.

### Side wear

Wheel flanges rubbing against the side of the rail head create side wear. This is a clear sign of misalignment or crane skewing.

### Joint problems

Rail joints that are uneven, open or poorly supported create impacts each time a wheel passes. Impacts damage wheel treads, bearings and the crane structure.

### Fixing failures

Loose or broken rail clips and bolts allow rails to move sideways or rock, causing misalignment and noise.

### Corrosion and contamination

Outdoor rails corrode; process debris and spillage on rails can cause wheels to slip or climb.

## How rail problems affect wheels

| Rail problem | Effect on wheels |
|---|---|
| Misaligned or out-of-gauge rails | Rapid flange wear, skewing |
| Uneven head wear | Uneven tread wear, spalling |
| Stepped or open joints | Impact damage, bearing wear |
| Loose fixings | Lateral movement, flange wear |
| Debris on rails | Tread damage, risk of climbing |
| Rail harder or softer than wheel | Accelerated wear of the softer component |

## What to inspect

**Isolate cranes and use proper access and fall protection before inspecting runways.**

- **Rail head profile:** look for uneven wear, flattening and side wear.
- **Joints:** check alignment, gaps, steps and joint bars or welds.
- **Clips and fixings:** check for missing, loose or broken components.
- **Pads and bedding:** where elastomeric pads are used, check for deterioration.
- **Alignment and gauge:** visually, and by periodic runway survey.
- **End stops:** secure and undamaged.
- **Cleanliness:** remove debris and spillage.

## Runway surveys

A runway survey measures rail straightness, span (gauge), elevation and joint condition along the full runway. Surveys are recommended:

- when wheel wear is excessive or uneven;
- after building modifications or settlement;
- after crane collisions;
- as part of long-term asset management for high-duty cranes.

Survey results show exactly where correction is needed, avoiding guesswork.

## Repair options

- **Re-fastening and shimming** to correct alignment and level.
- **Replacing clips and pads.**
- **Grinding or welding joints** to remove steps.
- **Replacing sections** of badly worn rail.
- **Full rail replacement** where wear is widespread.

## Match wheel and rail properties

Wheel hardness and profile should be compatible with the rail. Very hard wheels on softer rails wear the rails; soft wheels on hard rails wear quickly themselves. When replacing either, consider the other.

## Coordinate wheel and rail work

When planning wheel replacement:

1. Inspect the rails and review the runway survey.
2. Correct alignment and rail defects.
3. Fit wheels in matched sets.
4. Monitor wheel wear after the work to confirm the problem is solved.

When one crane wheel wears out, it can be tempting to replace only that wheel. On idler wheels that may be acceptable. On driven wheels it often creates a new problem: unequal wheel diameters that make the crane skew.

## The geometry of the problem

A driven wheel travels a distance equal to its circumference on every revolution. If two driven wheels on opposite sides of a bridge turn at the same speed but have different diameters, the larger wheel travels further. The bridge turns slightly with every revolution, forcing the crane to skew until flanges correct it — with friction, noise and wear.

Even small diameter differences matter because the effect accumulates over every metre the crane travels.

## Common drive arrangements

### Common drive (line shaft)

One motor drives wheels on both sides through a shaft. Both wheels turn at exactly the same speed, so diameter differences translate directly into skewing.

### Separate drives

Each end carriage has its own motor. Speeds are similar but not identical, and diameter differences still cause the crane to drift. Modern drive controls can partly compensate, but matched wheels remain important.

## Why mismatched wheels make wear worse

1. A new wheel is larger than the worn wheel opposite.
2. The crane skews towards the smaller wheel.
3. Flanges press against rails, wearing both.
4. Wear increases diameter differences further.
5. The cycle accelerates.

## Best practice for wheel replacement

| Situation | Recommended approach |
|---|---|
| One driven wheel worn | Replace the driven wheels on that drive line together |
| Common drive arrangement | Replace driven wheels as a matched set |
| Idler wheel worn | Can often be replaced individually, check overall alignment |
| Severe flange wear | Investigate alignment before replacing |

## How tight is "matched"?

Driven wheels in a set should be manufactured to closely controlled diameters. Your crane manufacturer or wheel supplier can specify an appropriate tolerance for the crane's design and speed. When ordering, request that driven wheels are supplied as a **matched set** with measured diameters recorded on the inspection report.

## Plan replacements in advance

Wheels wear gradually. By measuring wheel diameters during routine inspections, you can:

- predict when wheels will reach their limits;
- order matched sets with time to manufacture;
- replace wheels during planned shutdowns.

## Keep records

Record the measured diameter of every wheel at installation and at each inspection. This simple data reveals uneven wear early and helps diagnose skewing.

## Don't forget the cause

If wheels have worn unevenly or quickly, correct the cause — misalignment, drive imbalance, brake problems or runway defects — before fitting new wheels. Otherwise, the new matched set will soon become mismatched.

Every overhead and gantry crane uses travel drives to move the bridge along the runway (long travel) and the trolley across the bridge (cross travel). These drives include motors, gearboxes, couplings, brakes and controls. Their condition affects not only whether the crane moves, but how smoothly — and how fast wheels and rails wear.

## Drive arrangements

### Geared motors on each end carriage

Modern cranes typically use a geared motor mounted directly on each driven wheel or end carriage. This arrangement is compact and easy to maintain.

### Central drive with line shafts

Older cranes may use a single motor and gearbox driving wheels on both sides via long shafts and couplings.

### Trolley drives

Trolleys use similar arrangements on a smaller scale.

## Key components and common problems

### Motors

Problems include overheating, bearing failures and insulation breakdown. Frequent starting, inching and plugging place heavy demands on travel motors.

### Gearboxes

Gearboxes reduce motor speed and increase torque. Common issues include:

- oil leaks from seals;
- low or degraded oil;
- bearing wear and noise;
- gear tooth wear or damage from shock loads.

### Couplings

Couplings connect motors, gearboxes and shafts while allowing small misalignments. Flexible elements wear and need periodic replacement. Gear couplings require lubrication. Worn couplings cause noise, backlash and shock loading of gearboxes.

### Brakes

Travel brakes stop the crane and trolley. Uneven brake performance between sides contributes to skewing.

### Controls

Older cranes may use contactor control with resistors; modern cranes use variable frequency drives (VFDs) for smooth acceleration and deceleration.

## Maintenance checklist

**Isolate the crane and follow safe access procedures before work.**

- Check gearbox **oil levels and condition**; change oil at recommended intervals.
- Inspect for **oil leaks** and replace seals.
- Listen for **noise** and feel for heat on gearboxes and motors.
- Inspect **coupling elements** for wear and check alignment.
- Check **brake** linings and air gaps.
- Inspect **motor terminal boxes** and cables.
- Check **mounting bolts** and torque arms.
- Review **drive fault logs** on VFD-controlled cranes.

## Diagnostic quick reference

| Symptom | Possible causes |
|---|---|
| Clunk on starting or stopping | Coupling wear, gear backlash, loose mounting |
| Overheating motor | Excessive inching, brake dragging, overload |
| Noisy gearbox | Low oil, bearing wear, tooth damage |
| Crane skews on start | Unequal drives or brake timing |
| Oil on rails or brakes | Gearbox seal leaks |

## Upgrading to VFD control

Retrofitting VFDs to older cranes offers:

- smoother acceleration and braking;
- reduced shock on gearboxes and couplings;
- less load swing;
- reduced wheel and rail wear;
- better positioning control.

Upgrades should be engineered to suit the existing motors, brakes and structure.
