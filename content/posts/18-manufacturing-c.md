A component's material decides how strong it is, how well it resists wear and corrosion, and how it responds to machining, welding and heat treatment. Choosing well avoids premature failures; choosing poorly can make even a perfectly machined part fail early.

## Start with the demands

Before choosing a material, define what the component must withstand:

- **Loads:** static, cyclic (fatigue) and impact.
- **Wear:** sliding, rolling or abrasive contact.
- **Environment:** corrosion, temperature, chemicals.
- **Manufacturing:** machining, welding and heat treatment requirements.
- **Cost and availability.**

## Common material families

### Low-carbon structural steels

Easy to weld and fabricate; used for frames, brackets, base plates and general structures. They have moderate strength and are not significantly hardenable by heat treatment.

### Medium-carbon steels

Higher strength and hardenability. Widely used for shafts, pins, axles and piston rods, often with induction hardening or quench-and-temper treatment.

### Alloy steels

Contain alloying elements such as chromium, molybdenum and nickel. They offer higher strength, toughness and hardenability for highly stressed parts like heavily loaded shafts, gears and crane wheels.

### Stainless steels

Used where corrosion resistance is essential, such as marine and chemical environments. Grades vary widely in strength, corrosion resistance and machinability.

### Cast irons and cast steels

Used for housings, large wheels and complex shapes. Cast iron offers good machinability and vibration damping; cast steel offers higher toughness.

### Bronzes and bearing materials

Used for bushes, bearings and wear components that slide against steel, offering good wear resistance and compatibility with lubricants.

### Engineering plastics

Used for wear rings, guide strips and some bushes, offering low friction and corrosion resistance.

## Matching materials to components

| Component | Typical material approach |
|---|---|
| Fabricated frames and brackets | Weldable structural steel |
| Piston rods | Medium-carbon or alloy steel, induction hardened and chrome plated |
| Pins and axles | Medium-carbon or alloy steel, hardened as required |
| Crane wheels | Forged or cast steel with hardened treads |
| Cylinder barrels | Seamless or cold-drawn tubes suited to honing |
| Bushes | Bronze, hardened steel or engineering plastics |
| Gears | Alloy steels, through or surface hardened |
| Marine components | Stainless steels or coated carbon steels |

## Heat treatment changes everything

The same steel can behave very differently depending on heat treatment. **Normalising** refines grain structure; **quenching and tempering** increases strength and toughness; **induction hardening** produces hard surfaces with tough cores. Always specify heat treatment together with the material.

## Weldability

Higher carbon and alloy content generally makes steel harder to weld without cracking. Parts that must be welded may need lower-carbon grades or carefully controlled welding procedures, including preheat.

## Corrosion resistance

Options for corrosive environments include stainless steels, protective coatings, plating and paint systems. The choice depends on the severity of the environment, the required strength and the cost.

## Material certificates and traceability

For critical components, request **material test certificates** from the steel supplier. These document chemical composition and mechanical properties and support traceability.

## When reverse-engineering parts

Determine the original material through hardness testing and, where necessary, material analysis. Consider upgrading if the original part failed by wear or fatigue — a better material or heat treatment can significantly extend life.

Corrosion costs industry enormous amounts every year in replacement parts, downtime and safety risks. Ports, mines, chemical plants and outdoor installations are particularly harsh environments. Selecting the right surface treatment for each component is one of the most effective ways to extend equipment life.

## How corrosion attacks components

- **General corrosion:** uniform rusting of exposed steel surfaces.
- **Pitting:** localised attack creating deep pits, especially damaging on piston rods and sealing surfaces.
- **Crevice corrosion:** in gaps between parts where moisture is trapped.
- **Galvanic corrosion:** when dissimilar metals are in contact in the presence of moisture.
- **Corrosion fatigue:** cracking accelerated by the combination of cyclic stress and corrosion.

## Common surface treatments

### Paint systems

Multi-layer paint systems with suitable primers, intermediate coats and topcoats protect structures and fabricated parts. Surface preparation, such as abrasive blasting, is critical for adhesion and durability.

### Hot-dip galvanising

Steel is coated with zinc by immersion in molten zinc. Galvanising provides robust protection for structural steel, walkways and brackets, and the zinc protects the steel sacrificially even where scratched.

### Zinc and zinc-alloy plating

Electroplated zinc provides corrosion protection for fasteners and small parts, often with additional passivation layers.

### Hard chrome plating

Provides a hard, wear-resistant and corrosion-resistant surface for piston rods and wear surfaces. In very aggressive environments, standard chrome may need to be supplemented by other solutions.

### Electroless nickel

Deposits a uniform nickel layer, even on complex shapes and internal surfaces. Offers good corrosion and wear resistance.

### Chemical blackening and phosphating

Provide mild corrosion resistance and a base for oils or paints. Common on machined parts and fasteners.

### Thermal spray and cladding

Thermal spray coatings and weld or laser cladding apply corrosion- and wear-resistant materials to surfaces, used for rods, shafts and wear areas in severe service.

## Choosing a treatment

| Component | Typical treatment options |
|---|---|
| Structural frames | Blasting and multi-coat paint, or galvanising |
| Fasteners | Zinc plating, galvanising, stainless steel |
| Piston rods | Hard chrome; upgraded coatings for marine/chemical environments |
| Machined parts (internal) | Oiling, phosphating, blackening |
| Wear surfaces in corrosive environments | Electroless nickel, thermal spray, cladding |

## Design for corrosion resistance

Good design reduces corrosion risk:

- avoid water traps and crevices;
- provide drainage holes in structures;
- avoid contact between dissimilar metals or isolate them;
- allow access for inspection and maintenance;
- specify sealing of joints exposed to weather.

## Maintenance matters

Coatings need maintenance. Inspect paintwork for damage and touch up promptly. Clean salt and chemical deposits from equipment regularly. Protect exposed rods and machined surfaces during storage.

## Coatings and hydraulic seals

For hydraulic rods, any coating must be compatible with the seals and achieve the required surface finish. Not all corrosion-resistant coatings are suitable for dynamic sealing surfaces, so coating choice should be discussed with the cylinder manufacturer.

Not every part can be bought from a catalogue. Special machines, obsolete equipment and demanding applications often need custom components — a cylinder with an unusual mounting, a manifold for a specific circuit, or a replacement part with no available drawing. Understanding how custom manufacturing works helps you plan projects and avoid surprises.

## Step 1: Enquiry and requirements

Everything starts with clear requirements. A good enquiry includes:

- drawings, sketches, samples or photos;
- the function of the component and how it is used;
- loads, pressures, speeds and duty cycle;
- environment and any special conditions;
- quantities, now and in future;
- required documentation and testing;
- target delivery date.

The more information provided, the faster and more accurate the quotation.

## Step 2: Engineering review

Engineers review the requirements and identify:

- missing information;
- manufacturability issues;
- opportunities to simplify or improve the design;
- standard components that can reduce cost and lead time;
- risks, such as tight tolerances or unusual materials.

This is often where the most value is added. Small design changes can cut cost or improve reliability significantly.

## Step 3: Design and drawings

For new designs or reverse-engineered parts, drawings are prepared with dimensions, tolerances, materials, heat treatment and finishes. For complete hydraulic systems, a circuit diagram and component list are prepared.

## Step 4: Customer approval

The customer reviews and approves the drawings or design before manufacturing begins. This step prevents costly misunderstandings.

## Step 5: Prototype or first article

For new designs, a prototype or first article is manufactured and fully inspected. It may be tested on a rig or fitted to the machine for trial. Feedback leads to adjustments before production.

## Step 6: Testing and validation

Depending on the component, testing may include pressure tests, function tests, load tests, hardness checks and dimensional inspection. Results are documented.

## Step 7: Production

Once the design is validated, components are produced in the required quantities, with in-process and final inspection. CNC programs, fixtures and inspection plans developed during prototyping make repeat production consistent.

## Step 8: Documentation and delivery

Deliveries include inspection reports, test records, material certificates and drawings as agreed.

## Typical timeline factors

| Factor | Impact on lead time |
|---|---|
| Complete, clear information at enquiry | Shortens quotation and design time |
| Approval turnaround | Delays waiting for approval add directly to lead time |
| Special materials or bought-in components | May add procurement time |
| Heat treatment, plating and coatings | Add processing time |
| Testing requirements | Add time but reduce risk |

## How buyers can speed things up

1. Provide complete information and samples early.
2. Nominate a technical contact who can answer questions quickly.
3. Approve drawings promptly.
4. Agree documentation requirements at the start.
5. Order spares at the same time as the first units.

## Why it is worth it

Custom components fit the application exactly, solve problems standard parts cannot and can outperform originals when improvements are built in. With approved drawings on file, future orders become fast and simple.
