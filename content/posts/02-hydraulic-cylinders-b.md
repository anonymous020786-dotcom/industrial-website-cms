A failed hydraulic cylinder can stop a crane, a press line or a fleet of mobile machines. The repair cost is usually small compared with the cost of the downtime. The good news is that cylinders rarely fail without a reason, and the same causes appear again and again.

Here are the nine most common causes of hydraulic cylinder failure, the signs to look for, and how to prevent each one.

## 1. Contaminated hydraulic fluid

Contamination is the single biggest cause of hydraulic component failure. Hard particles act like grinding paste between the rod and seals, and between the piston and barrel. Water causes corrosion and degrades the oil.

**Signs:** scored rods and barrels, rapid seal wear, cloudy or dark oil.

**Prevention:** use the correct filtration, change filters on schedule, keep reservoir breathers in good condition, cap open hoses and ports during maintenance, and take periodic oil samples.

## 2. Side loading and misalignment

Cylinders are designed to push and pull in a straight line. When the load is offset or the mountings are misaligned, the rod is forced against the guide bushing on one side.

**Signs:** uneven wear on one side of the rod or bushing, oval wear patterns, leakage that returns quickly after resealing.

**Prevention:** check mounting alignment, use spherical bearings or self-aligning mountings where movement is expected, and replace worn pins and bushes before they allow excessive play.

## 3. Damaged piston rods

The rod is exposed to the environment every time it extends. Impacts from debris, weld spatter or careless handling dent or scratch the chrome. Every extension then drags the damage through the rod seal.

**Signs:** visible scratches, pits or flaking chrome; a rod seal that leaks soon after replacement.

**Prevention:** fit rod guards or bellows in harsh environments, protect rods during nearby welding, and inspect rods during routine checks. Light damage can sometimes be polished; deeper damage needs re-chroming or a new rod.

## 4. Wrong or worn seals

Seals must suit the pressure, temperature, speed and fluid. A seal that is not compatible with the fluid can swell or harden. A seal designed for lower pressure can extrude into the clearance gap.

**Signs:** leaks, drift, nibbled or extruded seal edges, hardened or cracked seals.

**Prevention:** use quality seal kits matched to the actual operating conditions, and replace all seals together during a rebuild rather than only the one that leaked.

## 5. Excessive heat

High oil temperature thins the fluid, increases internal leakage and shortens seal life dramatically. Many elastomer seals degrade quickly once temperatures rise above their rated limit.

**Signs:** hard, brittle or discoloured seals; sluggish performance on hot days or after long cycles.

**Prevention:** keep coolers clean, check relief valve settings, avoid running the system over relief, and investigate why oil is getting hot instead of just tolerating it.

## 6. Pressure spikes and shock loads

Sudden stops, dropped loads and fast valve shifts create pressure spikes far above normal working pressure. Over time these fatigue welds, threads and mountings.

**Signs:** cracks at welds or ports, loosened glands, damaged end caps, bent rods.

**Prevention:** use end-of-stroke cushioning where needed, set relief valves correctly, and train operators to avoid shock loading.

## 7. Rod buckling

Long-stroke cylinders under compression can buckle if the rod is too slender for the load, especially when the cylinder is extended and mounted with pivots at both ends.

**Signs:** a visibly bent rod, binding, and seal leakage at the gland.

**Prevention:** check buckling calculations during design, use larger rod diameters or stop tubes for long strokes, and do not exceed the rated load.

## 8. Corrosion

Moisture in the oil corrodes the barrel and piston from the inside. Outside, salt, chemicals and humidity attack the rod surface, especially on equipment that stands idle with rods extended.

**Signs:** rust spots or pitting on the rod, pitting inside the barrel found during repair.

**Prevention:** store equipment with rods retracted where possible, remove water from the oil, specify suitable rod coatings for marine or chemical environments and apply protective products to exposed rods.

## 9. Poor installation and maintenance practices

Many failures begin on the workshop bench: damaged seals during assembly, dirty components, incorrect torque on gland threads, or air left in the system.

**Signs:** failures shortly after repair or installation.

**Prevention:** assemble in clean conditions, use the correct installation tools, follow torque specifications, bleed air properly and pressure test after repair.

## A quick diagnostic table

| Symptom | Most likely causes |
|---|---|
| External leak at rod seal | Rod damage, worn or wrong seal, side load |
| Load drifts under pressure | Piston seal bypass, valve leakage |
| Jerky movement | Air in oil, stick-slip from worn seals, contamination |
| Rod scored along its length | Contamination, damaged wiper |
| Bent rod | Buckling, side load, impact |
| Repeated failure after rebuild | Root cause not fixed, alignment, installation errors |

## Treat the cause, not just the symptom

When a cylinder fails, ask *why* before ordering seals. A cylinder resealed without correcting misalignment or contamination will often fail again within weeks. A good repair shop should report what it found inside the cylinder — scored barrels, damaged seals, contamination — so you can fix the underlying problem.

Seals are small, inexpensive parts, yet they decide whether a hydraulic cylinder holds its load or leaks oil across the workshop floor. Choosing the wrong seal can turn a routine repair into a repeat failure. This guide explains the different hydraulic cylinder seal types, what each one does, and how to choose the right seal kit.

## The seals inside a typical cylinder

A standard double-acting cylinder contains several types of seal, each with a specific role.

### Rod seal

The rod seal sits in the gland and prevents oil escaping along the piston rod. It is the most critical seal for preventing external leakage. It must seal at full system pressure while the rod moves in and out, and it must allow a very thin oil film to lubricate the rod without leaving visible leakage.

### Wiper (scraper)

The wiper sits at the outer end of the gland. It does not hold pressure. Its job is to scrape dirt, dust and moisture off the rod as it retracts, so contamination does not reach the rod seal. A worn wiper is one of the most common reasons rod seals fail early.

### Piston seal

The piston seal stops oil passing from one side of the piston to the other. If it wears or is damaged, oil bypasses the piston, the cylinder loses force and loads can drift. Piston seals may be **single-acting** (sealing pressure from one side) or **double-acting** (sealing in both directions).

### Wear rings (guide rings)

Wear rings are fitted on the piston and in the gland. They keep metal parts apart and absorb side loads. They are not designed to seal, but without them the piston would contact the barrel and the rod would contact the gland, causing scoring.

### Static seals

O-rings and back-up rings seal joints that do not move, such as between the gland and the barrel or between the piston and the rod. Back-up rings prevent O-rings from extruding into clearance gaps at high pressure.

### Buffer seals

In higher-pressure or heavy-duty cylinders, a buffer seal is fitted ahead of the rod seal. It absorbs pressure peaks and protects the main rod seal, which then operates under lower, steadier pressure.

## Common seal materials

| Material | Strengths | Typical limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Polyurethane (PU) | Excellent wear and extrusion resistance, common in mobile hydraulics | Limited at high temperatures and with some water-based fluids |
| Nitrile rubber (NBR) | Good general-purpose material for mineral oils, widely used for O-rings | Moderate temperature range, less wear-resistant than PU |
| Fluoroelastomer (FKM) | High temperature and chemical resistance | Higher cost, less suited to low temperatures |
| PTFE compounds | Very low friction, high speed capability, no stick-slip | Usually needs an energiser ring; careful installation required |
| Polyacetal / filled nylons | Used for wear rings and back-up rings | Not sealing materials on their own |

Always confirm material compatibility with the actual hydraulic fluid. Fire-resistant and biodegradable fluids can be incompatible with materials that work well with mineral oil.

## How to choose the right seal kit

### 1. Pressure

Higher pressures increase the risk of extrusion, where the seal is forced into the clearance gap between moving parts. Harder materials, back-up rings and smaller clearances help.

### 2. Speed

Fast-moving rods generate heat at the seal lip. PTFE-based seals are often used at higher speeds because of their low friction.

### 3. Temperature

Consider both oil temperature and ambient temperature. Equipment in a foundry or steel plant needs very different seals from equipment working outdoors in cold climates.

### 4. Fluid type

Mineral oil, water-glycol, phosphate esters and biodegradable fluids each require compatible materials.

### 5. Environment

Dusty and muddy environments demand robust double-lip wipers. Corrosive environments may need special rod finishes alongside the seals.

### 6. Groove dimensions

A seal must match the groove it fits into. Replacing a seal with a "close enough" profile is a common cause of repeat leaks. If you do not have the original part numbers, measure the groove dimensions carefully or let the repairer measure them.

## Installation matters as much as selection

Even the right seal will fail if installed badly. Good practice includes:

- cleaning all components thoroughly before assembly;
- checking rods and barrels for burrs, sharp edges and damage;
- using installation tools and sleeves to protect seal lips from threads and edges;
- lubricating seals with clean system fluid before assembly;
- never twisting O-rings or overstretching seals;
- replacing the complete seal set rather than individual seals.

## When seals keep failing

If seals fail repeatedly, the seal is rarely the root cause. Look for scored rods or barrels, misalignment, contamination, excessive temperature or incorrect groove dimensions. A professional repair should identify these issues rather than simply fitting another seal kit.

When a hydraulic cylinder fails and the original drawings are missing, the replacement has to be built from measurements. Getting them right first time avoids delays, modifications and a second order. This guide walks you through every measurement a manufacturer needs.

## Before you start: safety

Never measure a cylinder that is still connected to a pressurised system. Lower or support the load mechanically, relieve system pressure, and disconnect the hydraulic lines with suitable caps on hoses and ports. Cylinders can contain trapped pressure, so follow your site's isolation procedures.

## Tools you will need

- Vernier caliper (and ideally an internal caliper or bore gauge)
- Steel tape measure
- Thread gauges for ports
- A camera to photograph mountings, ports and nameplates
- A notepad or measurement sheet

## 1. Check for a nameplate or part number

Many cylinders carry a stamped part number, serial number or manufacturer's plate. Photograph it before cleaning. If the machine manufacturer is known, their part number can save a lot of measuring.

## 2. Bore diameter

The bore is the internal diameter of the barrel. It cannot be measured directly without dismantling, so measure the **outside diameter of the barrel** and estimate wall thickness, or measure the bore during disassembly if the cylinder is being repaired. Tell the manufacturer which measurement you have taken.

## 3. Rod diameter

Measure the piston rod with a caliper at several points along its length. Wear and chrome damage can affect readings, so record the largest consistent figure. Common rod sizes follow standard increments, which helps confirm your measurement.

## 4. Stroke

Stroke is the distance the rod travels from fully retracted to fully extended. Measure the exposed rod length when fully retracted and again when fully extended; the difference is the stroke. If the cylinder cannot be cycled, the stroke can be determined during dismantling.

## 5. Retracted and extended lengths

These are measured between the **centres of the mounting points** — for example, pin centre to pin centre. Record:

- **Retracted length (closed centres)**
- **Extended length (open centres)**

These two dimensions are critical because the cylinder must fit the machine geometry exactly.

## 6. Mounting types and dimensions

Identify the mounting at each end and record its dimensions:

| Mounting | What to measure |
|---|---|
| Clevis | Pin diameter, slot width, clevis depth |
| Eye / spherical bearing | Pin diameter, eye width, bearing type |
| Trunnion | Trunnion diameter, length, position along the barrel |
| Flange | Flange thickness, bolt pattern, bolt sizes, pilot diameter |
| Tang or lug | Thickness, hole size, distance to centre |

Note whether eyes contain plain bushes or spherical bearings, and measure any grease nipple positions.

## 7. Port details

Record the **type, size and position** of each port:

- thread type and size (BSP, NPT, metric, SAE O-ring boss) or flange type;
- port orientation relative to the mountings;
- distance from the port centre to the end of the cylinder.

Photographs taken from several angles help the manufacturer understand port orientation.

## 8. Operating conditions

Measurements describe the geometry; operating conditions decide the design. Tell the manufacturer:

- working pressure and maximum pressure;
- whether the cylinder pushes, pulls or both;
- the load and any shock loading;
- operating speed and cycles per day;
- fluid type and temperature range;
- environment: outdoor, marine, dusty, hot or corrosive.

## 9. Why the original cylinder failed

If the cylinder failed, describe how. A bent rod might point to buckling or side loads; repeated leaks may point to misalignment. A replacement made to the same dimensions will fail the same way if the cause is not addressed.

## A simple measurement checklist

1. Part number / photos of nameplate
2. Bore (or barrel outside diameter)
3. Rod diameter
4. Stroke
5. Retracted and extended centres
6. Mounting types and dimensions at both ends
7. Port types, sizes, positions and orientation
8. Working pressure, load and speed
9. Fluid type, temperature and environment
10. Failure description and quantity required

## When measuring is difficult

Sometimes the easiest route is to send the cylinder, or the worn parts, to a manufacturer who can **reverse-engineer** it, produce a drawing for your approval and manufacture an exact replacement — often with improvements to address the original failure.
