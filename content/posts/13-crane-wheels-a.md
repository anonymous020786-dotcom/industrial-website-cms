Crane wheels carry the full weight of the crane and its load, rolling back and forth thousands of times a day on busy cranes. Some wear is inevitable. But wheels that wear quickly or unevenly are telling you something about the crane, the rails or how the crane is used.

## How crane wheels wear

Wheels wear in two main areas:

- **Tread wear:** the rolling surface that contacts the rail head.
- **Flange wear:** the raised edges that guide the wheel along the rail.

A small amount of tread wear over a long period is normal. Rapid flange wear is almost always a sign of an underlying problem.

## What wear patterns reveal

| Wear pattern | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| Heavy flange wear on one side of all wheels | Crane skewing or runway misalignment |
| Flange wear on diagonally opposite wheels | Wheel misalignment or skewing |
| Uneven tread wear across width | Wheel not perpendicular to rail, rail head wear |
| Flat spots on tread | Wheel sliding or skidding, brake problems |
| Spalling or flaking of tread | Overload, insufficient hardness, fatigue |
| Different diameters on driven wheels | Unequal wear causing skewing (cause and effect) |
| Rapid tread wear | Wheel too soft for duty, overload, poor rail condition |

## Common causes of excessive wear

### Misalignment

Wheels that are not square to the rail or not parallel to each other try to "steer" the crane, forcing the flanges against the rail. End carriages damaged by collisions are a common source of misalignment.

### Runway problems

Rails that are out of straightness, out of level, wrong gauge or poorly fastened push the crane sideways. Building settlement and thermal movement can alter runway geometry over time.

### Crane skewing

If one side of the bridge travels faster than the other, the crane skews. Unequal driven wheel diameters, unbalanced drives or slipping brakes can cause skewing.

### Material and hardness

Wheels must be hard enough for their load and duty. Too soft, and treads and flanges wear rapidly. Correct heat treatment provides a hard, wear-resistant surface with a tough core.

### Operating practice

Frequent plugging (reversing under power), harsh acceleration and braking, and collisions with end stops accelerate wear and damage.

## How to inspect crane wheels

**Always isolate the crane and use safe access before inspection.**

1. **Visual inspection:** look for flange wear, tread damage, cracks, spalling and flat spots.
2. **Measure flange thickness** with a gauge and compare against limits.
3. **Measure tread diameter** and compare driven wheels on each side.
4. **Check bearings:** listen for noise and check for play and heat.
5. **Check alignment:** wheel squareness and parallelism, ideally by survey during major maintenance.
6. **Inspect the rail:** head wear, alignment, joints and fixings.

## When to replace

Replace wheels when:

- flange thickness falls below the manufacturer's limit;
- tread wear exceeds allowable limits or creates diameter differences between driven wheels;
- cracks or significant spalling are found;
- bearings and axles are damaged beyond repair.

Driven wheels on the same rail line should be kept closely matched in diameter. Replacing them in **matched pairs** prevents new skewing problems.

## Fix the cause, then replace the wheel

Fitting new wheels on a misaligned crane or runway will repeat the problem — often faster, because new wheels have sharper flanges that bite into the rail. Before replacing wheels:

- survey runway alignment if wear is severe;
- check end carriage squareness;
- check drive balance and brakes;
- review operating practices.

## Specifying replacement wheels

Provide: tread diameter, width, flange type (single or double), rail type, bore and keyway or axle details, hardness requirements, crane capacity, and duty. Photos of the worn wheel and wear pattern help the manufacturer recommend improvements.

Crane wheels do more than carry load. Their flanges guide the crane along its rails and prevent derailment. Crane wheels are usually supplied in three basic profiles: **double flange**, **single flange** and **flangeless**. Each suits different applications.

## Double-flange wheels

Double-flange wheels have a flange on each side of the tread, capturing the rail head between them.

**Advantages**

- Positive guidance in both directions.
- Lower risk of derailment.
- The most common choice for overhead crane bridges and many trolleys.

**Considerations**

- The gap between flanges (clearance to the rail head) must suit the rail and allow for thermal movement and runway tolerances.
- If the runway is misaligned, flanges may bind and wear quickly.

## Single-flange wheels

Single-flange wheels have one flange, normally on the outside or inside of the rail depending on the design.

**Typical uses**

- Some underhung and top-running trolleys.
- Gantry cranes and transfer cars where guidance is provided on one side.
- Applications where one rail is the guide rail and the other allows lateral float.

**Considerations**

- Guidance relies on correct installation and rail layout.
- Lateral forces must be managed by the design.

## Flangeless wheels

Flangeless wheels have a plain tread and rely on separate **guide rollers** for guidance.

**Typical uses**

- Large spans where thermal expansion and runway tolerances would otherwise cause flange wear.
- Cranes with horizontal guide roller systems.
- Some heavy-duty process cranes.

**Considerations**

- Guide rollers must be maintained and correctly adjusted.

## Tread shape: cylindrical or tapered

Tread profile also matters. **Cylindrical treads** are common on many industrial cranes. **Tapered treads** are used on some drive arrangements to help the crane self-centre and reduce skewing, provided they are used with the appropriate rail head profile and drive configuration. Mixing tread types incorrectly can cause tracking problems.

## Choosing the right profile

| Consideration | Points towards |
|---|---|
| Standard overhead crane bridge | Double flange |
| Wide span, large thermal movement | Flangeless with guide rollers, or larger flange clearance |
| Guide rail on one side only | Single flange on guide side |
| Transfer car on floor rails | Double or single flange depending on layout |
| History of severe flange wear | Review alignment, consider guide rollers |

## Flange clearance

The clearance between the flange and the rail head is important. Too tight and flanges bind and wear; too loose and the crane can skew more before being corrected. Clearance is set by the crane designer based on span, rail size and runway tolerances.

## Replacing wheels: match the original — or improve it

When ordering replacements:

1. Confirm the flange type and positions.
2. Measure tread width and flange-to-flange distance.
3. Record the rail type and head width.
4. Note tread profile (cylindrical or tapered).
5. Describe any wear problems with the existing wheels.

If wheels have worn rapidly, discuss the wear pattern with the manufacturer. Improved hardness or a different flange arrangement — combined with alignment correction — may extend life considerably.

Two crane wheels can look identical and still have very different service lives. The difference lies in the material and heat treatment. Understanding these factors helps you specify replacement wheels that last, without damaging the rails they run on.

## The demands on a crane wheel

A crane wheel must:

- carry high **contact stresses** where the tread meets the rail;
- resist **wear** from rolling and sliding;
- resist **fatigue** from millions of load cycles;
- withstand **impacts** and occasional shock loads;
- remain **tough** enough not to crack.

These requirements pull in different directions. A very hard material resists wear but can be brittle. A tough material resists cracking but wears faster. Heat treatment gives the best of both: a hard surface with a tough core.

## Material options

### Forged steel

Forging refines the grain structure of the steel and minimises internal defects. Forged wheels are widely used for heavy-duty applications because of their strength and consistency.

### Cast steel

Cast wheels can be economical for larger wheels and complex shapes. Casting quality must be carefully controlled to avoid porosity and inclusions.

### Rolled or machined from bar

Smaller wheels are often machined from solid bar or forged blanks.

Common steels for crane wheels are medium-carbon and alloy steels that respond well to heat treatment. The exact grade depends on wheel size, load and duty.

## Heat treatment methods

### Through hardening

The entire wheel is heat-treated to a uniform hardness. This gives consistent properties but limits the maximum hardness that can be used without making the wheel brittle.

### Induction hardening of the tread

The tread and flanges are heated by induction and quenched, producing a **hard, wear-resistant surface layer** while the core remains tougher. The depth of hardening is important: it must be deep enough to support contact stresses and allow for wear.

### Flame hardening

Similar in principle to induction hardening, using a flame to heat the surface. Used for some larger wheels.

## Hardness and rail compatibility

Wheel hardness should be matched to the rail. If the wheel is much harder than the rail, the rail wears quickly; if it is much softer, the wheel wears quickly. Rails are expensive and disruptive to replace, so wheel specifications usually aim for a balanced relationship with the rail material.

## Typical choices by duty

| Duty | Typical approach |
|---|---|
| Light-duty cranes | Through-hardened or normalised steel |
| Moderate-duty cranes | Through-hardened or induction-hardened tread |
| Heavy and severe duty | Forged alloy steel with induction-hardened tread and flanges |

These are general guides; final specifications should consider load, wheel diameter, speed, rail type and environment.

## Quality checks to expect

When buying replacement wheels, ask for:

- **hardness test results** on the tread (and sometimes the flange);
- **hardness depth** information for surface-hardened wheels where required;
- **dimensional inspection report** covering diameter, width, bore and flange dimensions;
- **material certificates** where available.

## Signs the material or treatment is wrong

- Rapid, smooth tread wear: wheel too soft.
- Cracking or spalling: overload, insufficient case depth or brittle material.
- Rapid rail wear: wheel much harder than the rail.

## Getting the specification right

Provide the manufacturer with crane capacity, wheel load, duty, rail type, speed and the history of the existing wheels. With this information, material and heat treatment can be optimised for long life.
