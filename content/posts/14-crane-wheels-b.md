A healthy overhead crane travels straight down its runway with its bridge square to the rails. A skewing crane travels slightly diagonally, "crabbing" as it goes, with wheel flanges grinding against the rails. Skewing causes rapid wheel and rail wear, noise, structural stress and, in severe cases, derailment risk.

## Signs your crane is skewing

- Heavy flange wear on wheels, often on diagonally opposite corners.
- Shiny wear marks on the side of the rail heads.
- Squealing or grinding during travel.
- The crane "walks" to one side or the bridge is visibly out of square.
- Uneven wear on end stops and buffers.

## Common causes

### 1. Unequal wheel diameters

If driven wheels on one side are smaller than on the other, that side travels a shorter distance per revolution. The crane gradually skews. This is often both a cause and a result of skewing — wear creates diameter differences, which increases skew, which increases wear.

### 2. Drive imbalance

On cranes with separate drives on each end truck, differences in motor speed, brake timing or gear ratios make one side lead the other.

### 3. Runway misalignment

Rails that are not straight, not parallel, at the wrong gauge or at different elevations force the crane sideways. Causes include building settlement, damaged runway beams, loose rail clips and poor original installation.

### 4. Damaged or out-of-square end carriages

Collisions with end stops or other cranes can bend end carriages or shift wheel blocks, putting wheels out of alignment.

### 5. Brake problems

A travel brake that drags or releases late on one side causes uneven travel and skewing, especially during starting and stopping.

### 6. Operating practice

Aggressive acceleration and frequent reversing under power exaggerate skewing tendencies.

## How to diagnose

**Safety first:** isolate the crane and use proper access and fall protection for all inspections.

1. **Inspect wear patterns** on all wheels and rails, noting which flanges are worn and on which side.
2. **Measure driven wheel diameters** and compare.
3. **Check drives and brakes** for equal performance on both sides.
4. **Check end carriage squareness** and wheel alignment.
5. **Commission a runway survey** to measure rail straightness, span, elevation and joints along the full length.

A runway survey gives objective data and is the foundation for correcting serious alignment problems.

## Solutions

| Cause | Solution |
|---|---|
| Unequal driven wheel diameters | Replace driven wheels in matched sets |
| Drive imbalance | Check and match drives, brakes and controls |
| Runway misalignment | Realign rails, repair runway beams, replace clips |
| Bent end carriages | Repair or replace, re-square wheels |
| Brake issues | Adjust or repair brakes, balance timing |
| Operating practice | Operator training, soft-start drives |

## Variable speed drives help

Modern variable frequency drives (VFDs) provide smooth acceleration and deceleration, reducing skewing forces. They are a common upgrade for older cranes with direct-on-line or resistance-controlled travel motors.

## Guide rollers for long spans

For wide-span cranes or runways with thermal movement, horizontal guide rollers can take guidance forces off the wheel flanges. This is a design change that should be engineered by qualified crane specialists.

## Do it in the right order

Replacing worn wheels without correcting the runway or drives will lead to the same wear again. A sensible sequence is: survey → correct runway and end carriages → balance drives → fit matched new wheels → monitor wear.

The end carriage — also called an end truck — sits at each end of an overhead crane's bridge. It carries the bridge girders, houses the travel wheels and supports the long travel drives. Every movement of the crane along its runway depends on the condition of the end carriages.

## Main components

- **Frame:** a fabricated box or channel structure connecting to the bridge girders.
- **Wheels:** driven and idler wheels that run on the runway rails.
- **Axles and bearings:** support the wheels and allow rotation.
- **Bearing housings or wheel blocks:** hold bearings and wheels in alignment.
- **Drive units:** geared motors or motors with gearboxes driving the wheels.
- **Brakes:** often integrated with drive motors.
- **Buffers:** absorb impacts at the end of travel.
- **Rail sweepers and guards:** clear debris from the rail and protect against wheel failures.

## How end carriages wear

### Wheels

Tread and flange wear is the most common issue. Rapid wear usually indicates alignment or skewing problems.

### Bearings

Bearings wear from load, contamination and inadequate lubrication. Failing bearings produce noise, heat and play, and eventually allow wheels to tilt.

### Bearing housings and axles

Worn bores and axle seats let wheels move out of alignment. Fretting where bearings sit on axles is a common long-term problem.

### Frames

Collisions with end stops or other cranes can bend frames and disturb wheel alignment. Fatigue cracks may develop at welded connections on heavily used cranes.

### Drives

Gearbox wear, coupling wear and brake issues affect travel performance.

## Inspection checklist

**Isolate the crane and use safe access for all inspections.**

- Wheel flange and tread wear.
- Bearing noise, temperature and play.
- Wheel alignment and squareness.
- Frame condition: cracks, bending, corrosion.
- Bolted connections to the girders.
- Drive gearbox oil levels and leaks.
- Brake condition and adjustment.
- Buffers and rail sweepers present and undamaged.

## Repair or replace the whole assembly?

Individual wheels and bearings can be replaced when the frame and housings are sound. However, a complete replacement or remanufactured assembly may be better when:

- housings and axle seats are worn, making alignment difficult;
- frames are bent or cracked;
- the crane is being upgraded with new drives;
- multiple components are near the end of their life;
- downtime must be minimised — a complete assembly can be pre-built and installed quickly.

## Common upgrades

| Upgrade | Benefit |
|---|---|
| Hardened forged wheels | Longer wheel life on heavy-duty cranes |
| Modern wheel blocks with cartridge bearings | Easier alignment and maintenance |
| Variable frequency drives | Smoother travel, less skewing and wear |
| Improved buffers | Reduced impact damage |
| Rail sweepers and safety lugs | Protection against debris and wheel failure |

## Pre-assembled wheel sets save downtime

Supplying wheels pre-assembled with axles, bearings and housings means maintenance teams can swap complete units during a short shutdown rather than pressing bearings on site.

- Crane capacity, span and duty.
- Wheel diameter, flange type and rail size.
- Wheelbase and mounting interface to the bridge.
- Drive type and arrangement.
- Photos and dimensions of the existing end carriage.

Crane wheel bearings carry the crane's weight and every load it lifts, while allowing wheels to roll smoothly. A failed wheel bearing can cause wheel misalignment, skewing, overheating and, in the worst cases, wheel seizure. Good bearing selection and maintenance are essential for reliable crane travel.

## Common bearing types

### Spherical roller bearings

Widely used in crane wheel blocks because they handle high radial loads, tolerate some misalignment and carry moderate axial loads from guidance forces.

### Tapered roller bearings

Handle combined radial and axial loads well and are used in some wheel designs, usually in pairs with careful adjustment.

### Cylindrical roller bearings

High radial load capacity; often used in combination with other bearings that take axial loads.

### Deep groove ball bearings

Used for lighter-duty wheels and trolleys.

The right choice depends on wheel load, speed, axial forces, misalignment and space.

## Lubrication

Most crane wheel bearings are grease-lubricated. Good practice includes:

- using the **grease type specified** by the bearing or crane manufacturer;
- following **re-lubrication intervals** based on duty and environment;
- **not over-greasing**, which can cause overheating and seal damage;
- using clean grease and clean grease guns to avoid introducing contamination;
- ensuring grease reaches the bearing through clear lubrication paths.

High-temperature environments, such as steel plants, need greases suitable for elevated temperatures.

## Sealing

Seals keep grease in and contamination out. Dust, scale, water and process debris are the enemies of bearings. Inspect seals for damage and replace them when bearings are serviced. Outdoor and dusty environments benefit from improved sealing arrangements.

## Warning signs of bearing failure

| Sign | What it may indicate |
|---|---|
| Noise (rumbling, grinding, clicking) | Wear, damage, contamination |
| Heat at the wheel block | Lubrication problems, overload, failure |
| Wheel wobble or axial play | Bearing wear or loose fit |
| Grease leaking or discoloured | Seal failure, overheating |
| Uneven wheel wear | Misalignment caused by bearing wear |

Thermal imaging during operation is an easy way to spot bearings running hotter than their neighbours.

## Why bearings fail early

- **Poor lubrication:** too little, too much or the wrong grease.
- **Contamination:** dust, water and process debris.
- **Incorrect fitting:** damage during installation, wrong fits on shafts and housings.
- **Misalignment:** bent axles or worn housings.
- **Overload and shock:** collisions and harsh operation.

## Best practices for replacement

1. Replace bearings using **proper tools** — induction heaters and pullers rather than hammers and flames.
2. **Check shaft and housing fits** and repair worn seats.
3. **Clean all components** before assembly.
4. **Fit new seals** with new bearings.
5. **Pack with the correct grease quantity.**
6. **Check alignment** after reassembly.
