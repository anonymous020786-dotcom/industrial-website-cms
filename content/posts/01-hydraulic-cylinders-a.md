Hydraulic cylinders are the muscles of heavy equipment. They lift crane booms, tip dump bodies, clamp workpieces in presses and steer mining trucks. Despite doing very different jobs, almost every cylinder works on the same simple principle — and once your maintenance team understands that principle, diagnosing problems becomes much more logical.

This guide explains how hydraulic cylinders work, what each component does, and how to estimate the force and speed a cylinder can deliver.

## The basic principle: pressure acting on an area

A hydraulic cylinder converts **fluid pressure** into **linear force**. Oil from a pump enters the cylinder and pushes against a piston. Because hydraulic oil is almost incompressible, the pressure acts evenly across the whole face of the piston and the piston moves, pushing the rod out.

The relationship is straightforward:

**Force = Pressure × Area**

Double the pressure and you double the force. Double the piston area and you also double the force. This is why a relatively compact cylinder can move loads of many tonnes: system pressures of 150–250 bar acting on a large bore generate very high forces.

## The main components of a hydraulic cylinder

Every cylinder design varies, but you will find these parts in almost all of them:

- **Barrel (tube):** the main body. Its inner surface is honed to a fine finish so the piston seals can slide without wearing quickly.
- **Piston:** separates the two chambers inside the barrel and carries the piston seals and wear bands.
- **Piston rod:** transmits force from the piston to the load. Rods are usually induction-hardened and hard-chrome plated to resist wear, corrosion and impact damage.
- **Gland (head):** closes the rod end of the barrel and holds the rod seal, wiper and guide bushing.
- **End cap (base):** closes the other end of the barrel and usually includes a mounting.
- **Seals and wear rings:** keep oil where it belongs and guide moving parts so metal does not touch metal.
- **Ports:** where oil enters and leaves each chamber.
- **Mountings:** clevises, trunnions, flanges or eyes that connect the cylinder to the machine.

## Single-acting vs double-acting cylinders

**Single-acting cylinders** use hydraulic pressure in one direction only. The return stroke relies on gravity, the load itself or an internal spring. They are common in jacks, simple lifting devices and some tipping applications.

**Double-acting cylinders** have ports at both ends, so oil can push the piston in either direction. This gives powered extension and retraction and much better control, which is why most industrial and mobile machines use them.

## Push force vs pull force

In a double-acting cylinder, the extend (push) force and the retract (pull) force are different. When extending, pressure acts on the full piston area. When retracting, it acts only on the **annulus** — the piston area minus the rod area.

| Stroke direction | Effective area | Relative force | Relative speed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Extend (push) | Full piston area | Higher | Slower |
| Retract (pull) | Piston area − rod area | Lower | Faster |

This matters when you size a cylinder. If your application needs a high pulling force, you may need a larger bore than a push-only calculation suggests.

## Worked example: estimating cylinder force

Consider a cylinder with a 100 mm bore and a 56 mm rod operating at 200 bar.

1. Piston area = π × (0.1 m)² ÷ 4 ≈ 0.00785 m²
2. Pressure = 200 bar = 20,000,000 Pa
3. Push force ≈ 0.00785 × 20,000,000 ≈ 157,000 N, or roughly **16 tonnes-force**

For the pull force, subtract the rod area (π × 0.056² ÷ 4 ≈ 0.00246 m²) to get an annulus of about 0.00539 m², which gives roughly 108,000 N — about **11 tonnes-force**.

These are theoretical figures. In practice you should allow for friction, pressure losses in hoses and valves, and a sensible safety margin.

## How fast will the cylinder move?

Speed depends on how much oil flows into the cylinder:

**Speed = Flow ÷ Effective area**

With 40 litres per minute flowing into the 100 mm bore cylinder above, the extension speed is about 0.085 m/s, or roughly 85 mm per second. Because the annulus area is smaller, the same flow will retract the rod faster.

This is why some circuits use **regeneration**, routing oil from the rod side back into the cap side to increase extension speed at the cost of force.

## What limits a cylinder's performance?

Several practical factors decide whether a cylinder performs as calculated:

- **Rod buckling:** long, slender rods under compression can buckle. Longer strokes need thicker rods or additional guidance.
- **Seal friction and leakage:** worn seals reduce efficiency and can cause drift.
- **Side loading:** cylinders are designed to push and pull in a straight line. Misalignment loads the guide bushing and wears seals rapidly.
- **Cushioning:** without end-of-stroke cushioning, heavy loads slam into the end caps, causing shock and fatigue.
- **Contamination:** dirty oil scores the barrel and rod and destroys seals.

## Maintenance basics for longer cylinder life

You can extend cylinder life considerably with a few simple habits:

1. **Inspect rods regularly** for scratches, pitting and chrome damage. A damaged rod will quickly destroy new seals.
2. **Check for external leaks** at the gland and ports and investigate the cause rather than just topping up oil.
3. **Watch for drift.** A load that creeps down under pressure often points to internal leakage across the piston seal, although valves can also be responsible.
4. **Keep the oil clean** with proper filtration and sealed reservoirs.
5. **Check mountings and pins** for wear that allows misalignment.
6. **Protect exposed rods** on equipment that stands idle in harsh environments.

## When to repair and when to replace

Many cylinder faults can be repaired: seals can be replaced, rods re-chromed or remanufactured, and barrels re-honed within limits. Replacement becomes the better option when the barrel is bulged or worn beyond its tolerance, the rod is bent, or the design no longer suits the application.

## Key takeaways

- Hydraulic cylinders convert pressure into force using **Force = Pressure × Area**.
- Push force is higher than pull force because the rod reduces the effective area on the return stroke.
- Speed depends on oil flow and effective area.
- Clean oil, good alignment and undamaged rods are the foundation of long cylinder life.

When you specify a new hydraulic cylinder, one of the first decisions is the type of construction. The two most common designs are **tie-rod** and **welded** cylinders. Both convert hydraulic pressure into linear force in the same way, but the way they are built affects their size, strength, serviceability and cost.

This article compares the two designs and explains where each one makes most sense.

## What is a tie-rod cylinder?

A tie-rod cylinder holds its end caps onto the barrel using **high-strength threaded steel rods** that run along the outside of the barrel. Nuts on the ends of the tie rods clamp the assembly together.

Tie-rod cylinders are very common in **industrial applications** such as machine tools, presses, production lines and material handling equipment. Many are built to recognised dimensional standards, which makes interchangeability easier.

### Advantages of tie-rod cylinders

- **Easy to service:** remove the tie-rod nuts and the cylinder comes apart, so seals can be replaced quickly.
- **Standardised dimensions:** many sizes follow industry standards, which simplifies replacement.
- **Cost-effective** for standard bores and strokes at moderate pressures.

### Limitations of tie-rod cylinders

- **Larger outer dimensions:** the tie rods add width, which can be a problem where space is tight.
- **Pressure limits:** under high or shock loads, tie rods can stretch, which may lead to leakage at the end caps.
- **Long strokes:** very long tie rods can whip or need intermediate supports.
- **Dirt traps:** exposed tie rods collect debris in dirty environments.

## What is a welded cylinder?

A welded cylinder has its end cap **welded directly to the barrel**. The gland at the rod end is usually threaded or retained with a wire ring or bolted flange so the cylinder can still be dismantled for repair.

Welded cylinders dominate **mobile hydraulics** — cranes, excavators, tippers, agricultural machinery and mining equipment — and are widely used in heavy industrial machinery.

### Advantages of welded cylinders

- **Compact:** no external tie rods, so the cylinder is narrower for the same bore.
- **Robust:** better suited to high pressures, shock loads and harsh environments.
- **Flexible design:** mountings, ports and bores can be customised to fit the machine.
- **Long strokes:** no tie rods to stretch or support.

### Limitations of welded cylinders

- **Repair takes more specialist work:** glands can be very tight after years in service and need proper tooling to remove.
- **Usually custom-made:** less dimensional standardisation, so replacements are often manufactured to drawing or sample.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Factor | Tie-rod cylinder | Welded cylinder |
|---|---|---|
| Typical use | Industrial, factory automation | Mobile, heavy industrial |
| Pressure and shock tolerance | Moderate | High |
| Envelope size | Larger | Compact |
| Serviceability | Very easy | Good, with proper tooling |
| Standardisation | Often standardised | Usually custom |
| Harsh or dirty environments | Fair | Very good |
| Long strokes | Limited | Well suited |

## How to choose the right design

Ask these questions when specifying a cylinder:

1. **What is the working pressure and are there shock loads?** High pressure or impact usually points towards a welded design.
2. **How much space is available?** If width is limited, a welded cylinder is often the only option.
3. **Where will it operate?** Outdoor, dusty or wet environments favour welded construction without exposed tie rods.
4. **How important is fast maintenance?** On a production line with spare cylinders on the shelf, a standard tie-rod design can be swapped and resealed quickly.
5. **Does it need to match an existing standard?** If you are replacing an existing standard tie-rod cylinder, staying with the same type avoids machine modifications.

## What about other designs?

Tie-rod and welded are not the only options:

- **Telescopic cylinders** use nested stages to achieve a long stroke from a short retracted length, typically in tippers and dump bodies.
- **Mill-type cylinders** use bolted flanges and heavy construction for steel mills and severe industrial service.
- **Compact or short-stroke cylinders** suit clamping and fixturing.

## Repairing or replacing an existing cylinder

If you are replacing a cylinder, start by recording the **bore, rod diameter, stroke, retracted length, mounting type, port sizes and working pressure**. Photographs of the nameplate, mountings and ports help a manufacturer understand the application. If the cylinder has failed in service, tell your supplier how it failed — the cause may change the specification.

## Summary

- Choose **tie-rod cylinders** for standard industrial applications where serviceability and standard dimensions matter.
- Choose **welded cylinders** for mobile, high-pressure, shock-loaded or space-limited applications.
- Whatever the design, accurate dimensions and honest information about the operating conditions are the key to a cylinder that lasts.

Some applications need a very long stroke but have little space to mount a cylinder. A dump truck body, for example, must tip steeply, yet the cylinder has to fit under the body when it is lowered. The answer is the **telescopic hydraulic cylinder**.

This article explains how telescopic cylinders work, where they are used and how to keep them in good condition.

## How a telescopic cylinder works

A telescopic cylinder contains a series of **nested tubes, called stages**, each sliding inside the next. When oil enters the base, the largest stage extends first, followed by progressively smaller stages. When fully extended, the cylinder can be several times longer than its retracted length.

Because each stage has a different effective area, the cylinder behaves differently through its stroke:

- **The largest stage extends first** with the highest force and the lowest speed.
- **Smaller stages follow**, extending faster but with less force for the same pressure.

This suits tipping applications well. The most force is needed at the start of the lift, when the load is heaviest and the geometry is least favourable.

## Single-acting vs double-acting telescopic cylinders

**Single-acting telescopic cylinders** extend hydraulically and retract under gravity. They are by far the most common type, especially in tippers, trailers and dump bodies.

**Double-acting telescopic cylinders** can be powered in both directions. They are used where gravity cannot be relied on to retract the cylinder — for example in horizontal applications, push-off trailers or some refuse vehicles. They are more complex and need careful design of the internal oil paths.

## Typical applications

- Tipper trucks and dump trailers
- Agricultural trailers
- Refuse collection vehicles
- Mining and quarry haul equipment
- Some lifting platforms and specialised cranes
- Roll-off container systems

## Design considerations

Telescopic cylinders need careful engineering:

1. **Stability at full extension:** an extended telescopic cylinder is long and slender. The design must consider buckling, mounting geometry and side loads from uneven loading.
2. **Stage overlap:** each stage needs enough overlap with the next to resist bending. Too little overlap leads to wear and seal failure.
3. **Stop rings and cushioning:** stages must stop positively at the end of their travel without shock damage.
4. **Sealing:** every stage has its own seals, so there are more potential leak paths than in a standard cylinder.
5. **Mountings:** trunnion and eye mountings must allow the cylinder to follow the arc of the tipping body without binding.

## Common problems with telescopic cylinders

| Symptom | Possible causes |
|---|---|
| Stages extend out of sequence | Internal leakage, contamination, damaged stop rings or uneven wear |
| Jerky movement | Air in the system, worn bearings or contamination |
| External leaks between stages | Worn or damaged stage seals, scored stage surfaces |
| Body creeps down | Internal leakage, valve problems or worn seals |
| Bent stages | Side loading from uneven loads or tipping on slopes |

## Safety first: side loads and tipping on slopes

Many telescopic cylinder failures are not caused by the cylinder at all. **Tipping on uneven ground**, sticky loads that do not slide out, or uneven loading can put large side loads on an extended cylinder. Operators should tip only on firm, level ground and make sure loads discharge freely. Never work under a raised body unless it is supported by a suitable mechanical prop.

## Maintenance practices that extend life

- **Inspect every stage** for scoring, dents and chrome damage when the cylinder is extended for checks.
- **Keep stages clean.** Mud and grit on the stages are dragged past the wipers and seals.
- **Lubricate mounting points** as specified so the cylinder can pivot freely.
- **Check for leaks** at each stage seal and the bleed points.
- **Bleed air** from the system as recommended by the manufacturer.
- **Maintain clean oil** — telescopic cylinders are particularly sensitive to contamination because of their many sliding surfaces.

## Repair and reconditioning

Telescopic cylinders can usually be reconditioned. Typical work includes resealing every stage, polishing or re-plating damaged stages, replacing bearings and stop rings, and pressure testing the complete cylinder. Bent stages normally need replacing rather than straightening.

When ordering a replacement or repair, provide the number of stages, retracted and extended lengths, the largest stage diameter, mounting details and operating pressure.
