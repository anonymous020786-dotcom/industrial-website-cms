When an overhead crane stops, production often stops with it. The difference between a two-hour repair and a two-week shutdown is frequently whether the right spare part is available. Yet holding every possible part is expensive. A sensible spares strategy focuses on parts that are **critical, likely to wear and slow to obtain**.

## How to prioritise crane spares

Score each potential spare on three questions:

1. **Criticality:** if this part fails, does the crane stop or become unsafe?
2. **Failure likelihood:** is this a wear part, or one that fails occasionally?
3. **Lead time:** how quickly can a replacement be obtained or manufactured?

Parts that score high on all three belong on the shelf.

## Commonly stocked crane spare parts

### Hoist components

- **Wire rope** of the correct construction and length (or a supplier able to deliver quickly).
- **Brake linings, pads or complete brake units** for hoist brakes.
- **Limit switches** for upper and lower hoist limits.
- **Hook latches** and safety catches.
- **Sheave bearings** and, for high-duty cranes, spare sheaves.

### Travel components

- **Crane wheels**, or matched wheel sets for critical cranes.
- **Wheel bearings and bearing housings.**
- **Couplings and coupling elements** for travel drives.
- **Travel brake components.**

### Electrical components

- **Contactors and relays** used in crane control panels.
- **Pendant or radio remote controls** and spare batteries.
- **Fuses and protection devices.**
- **Festoon trolleys or conductor bar collectors.**
- **Motor brake coils and rectifiers.**

### Mechanical fasteners and consumables

- Bolts, pins and retaining devices specified for crane use.
- Lubricants specified by the crane manufacturer.
- Buffers and end stops.

## Wear parts vs insurance spares

**Wear parts** — brake linings, collectors, rope, wheel bearings — are consumed at a predictable rate. Stock levels can be based on usage history.

**Insurance spares** — gearboxes, motors, complete wheel assemblies — rarely fail, but when they do, lead times can be long. For critical cranes, holding one insurance spare can be cheaper than a single extended shutdown.

## When original parts are obsolete

Many cranes outlive their manufacturer's parts support. When original spares are no longer available, components such as wheels, pins, sheaves, couplings and drums can often be **reverse-engineered** from the worn part and manufactured to drawing. Doing this proactively — before the part fails — removes a major risk.

## Build a spares register

A simple register makes your strategy work:

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Crane / location | Bay 3 EOT, 20 t |
| Component | Long travel wheel, driven |
| Drawing / part number | Supplier part no. or in-house drawing |
| Quantity on hand / minimum | 2 / 2 |
| Lead time | 6 weeks (manufactured) |
| Supplier | Approved manufacturer |
| Last used | Date and reason |

## Store spares correctly

- Protect machined surfaces from corrosion with suitable preservatives.
- Store wire rope off the floor in a dry place.
- Keep brake linings and rubber parts away from heat, sunlight and ozone.
- Rotate stock where shelf life matters.
- Label parts clearly with crane, location and part details.

## Review regularly

Review the register after every breakdown and at least annually. Add parts that caused unplanned downtime, adjust quantities based on usage, and remove items for equipment that has been retired.

Electric overhead travelling (EOT) cranes are central to many factories, workshops and warehouses. Regular inspection keeps them safe and reliable, and in many countries periodic thorough examination by a competent person is a legal requirement.

This checklist is a practical starting point. Always follow the crane manufacturer's instructions and the regulations that apply in your country, and ensure inspections are carried out by suitably trained and competent people.

**Safety first:** before any inspection or maintenance, isolate and lock off the crane's electrical supply, prevent other cranes on the same runway from approaching, and use safe access equipment and fall protection.

## Daily (pre-use) checks — operator

Before each shift, the operator should check:

- **Controls:** pendant or remote buttons operate correctly and return to neutral; emergency stop works.
- **Hoist limits:** upper limit switch stops the hook safely.
- **Brakes:** hoist and travel brakes hold and stop smoothly without excessive drift.
- **Hook:** no visible damage, deformation or cracks; safety latch is present and works.
- **Wire rope:** no obvious kinks, broken wires, crushing or birdcaging along visible lengths.
- **Unusual noise or movement:** grinding, squealing or jerky travel.
- **Warning devices:** horns and lights work.
- **Area:** runway clear of obstructions.

Any defect should be reported and the crane taken out of service if safety is affected.

## Monthly inspection — maintenance team

- **Wire rope:** detailed inspection for wear, corrosion, broken wires and lubrication.
- **Sheaves and drum:** groove wear, rope seating, bearing noise.
- **Brakes:** lining wear, air gap adjustment, holding performance.
- **Hook block:** bearing rotation, wear at the hook saddle and shank.
- **Wheels and rails:** flange wear, tread wear, rail condition and fastenings.
- **Gearboxes:** oil levels, leaks, noise and temperature.
- **Couplings:** wear of flexible elements and alignment.
- **Electrical:** collectors, festoon cables, panel components and terminations.
- **Lubrication:** in line with the manufacturer's schedule.
- **Bolts and fastenings:** end trucks, girder connections and trolley frames.
- **Buffers and end stops:** secure and undamaged.

## Annual (or statutory) thorough examination

A competent person carries out a thorough examination covering the structure, mechanisms, safety devices and documentation, typically including:

- structural inspection of girders, end carriages and connections for cracks and deformation;
- detailed inspection of hoist drum, rope anchorages and reeving;
- verification of limit switches, overload protection and emergency stops;
- inspection of wheels, axles and bearings;
- brake performance verification;
- review of maintenance records;
- load testing where required by regulations or after major repairs.

## Record keeping

Keep a log for each crane with inspection dates, findings, repairs, parts replaced and examination certificates. Records demonstrate compliance and reveal trends, such as accelerating wheel wear that may indicate alignment problems.

## Common findings and what they mean

| Finding | Possible cause |
|---|---|
| Rapid wheel flange wear | Rail misalignment, crane skewing |
| Brake slip or long stopping distance | Worn linings, incorrect adjustment, contamination |
| Rope wear on one side | Sheave misalignment, worn grooves |
| Hot gearbox | Low oil, wrong oil, bearing wear |
| Trolley noise | Worn wheels, bearings or rail joints |

## Parts and repairs

When inspections reveal worn wheels, sheaves, pins or brake parts, plan replacements before they become breakdowns. Sheaves guide the wire rope through a crane's hoist system. Every lift bends the rope around each sheave, and the condition of the sheave groove directly affects how long the rope lasts. A worn or damaged sheave can ruin a new rope in a fraction of its normal life — so sheave inspection belongs in every crane maintenance routine.

## Why sheave condition matters

A wire rope is designed to sit in a groove that supports it around roughly a third of its circumference. When the groove is the correct size and smooth, load is spread evenly across the rope's wires.

When the groove wears, two things happen:

- **A groove that is too narrow** pinches the rope, crushing it and causing internal wire breaks.
- **A groove that is too wide or corrugated** fails to support the rope, letting it flatten and wear unevenly.

## What to inspect

### Groove diameter and profile

Use a sheave groove gauge suited to the rope diameter. The gauge should fit the groove bottom correctly. Grooves that are undersize, oversize or misshapen need attention.

### Corrugation

A worn rope can imprint its strand pattern into the groove, leaving corrugations. A new rope running in a corrugated groove will be damaged quickly. Corrugated sheaves must be machined (if within limits) or replaced.

### Flanges

Check sheave flanges for chips, cracks and wear. Damaged flanges can allow the rope to jump the sheave — a serious hazard.

### Bearings

The sheave should rotate freely and quietly without wobble. Seized or rough bearings make the rope slide across the groove, causing rapid wear on both rope and sheave.

### Alignment

Sheaves should be aligned with the rope path. Misalignment makes the rope rub against the flange and twist as it runs.

### Cracks

Look for cracks in the hub, spokes and web, particularly on cast sheaves and after shock loading.

## Common sheave problems and causes

| Problem | Typical causes |
|---|---|
| Undersize groove | Wear from rope, wrong sheave for rope size |
| Corrugated groove | Long use with worn rope |
| Flange wear on one side | Misalignment, fleet angle problems |
| Rough rotation | Bearing failure, lack of lubrication |
| Cracks | Overload, shock loads, fatigue |

## Sheave replacement or repair

Lightly worn grooves can sometimes be re-machined to the correct profile, provided enough material remains and the sheave design allows it. Sheaves with cracks, excessive groove wear, damaged flanges or worn bores should be replaced.

Replacement sheaves must match:

- the rope diameter and groove profile;
- the pitch diameter required by the crane design;
- bore and bearing arrangement;
- material and hardness requirements.

## Replace ropes and sheaves together when needed

When replacing a wire rope, inspect every sheave and the drum. Fitting a new rope on worn sheaves is a false economy, because the rope will start wearing immediately.

## Inspection frequency

Sheaves are typically checked during monthly crane inspections and in detail during the periodic thorough examination. High-duty cranes, such as those in steel plants or ports, may need more frequent checks.

## Safety reminder

Sheave inspection involves working at height near the hoist mechanism. Isolate the crane, secure the load block, and use appropriate access equipment.
