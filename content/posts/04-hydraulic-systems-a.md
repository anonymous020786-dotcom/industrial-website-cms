If hydraulic cylinders are the muscles of a machine, the hydraulic power pack is its heart. Also called a hydraulic power unit (HPU), a power pack stores hydraulic fluid, pressurises it, controls it and sends it to the actuators that do the work.

This guide explains what a hydraulic power pack is, the main components and how they work together.

## What does a hydraulic power pack do?

A power pack converts mechanical energy — usually from an electric motor or engine — into hydraulic energy in the form of pressurised fluid flow. That flow is directed by valves to cylinders and hydraulic motors, which convert it back into mechanical work.

Power packs range from small units the size of a shoebox powering a single clamp, to skid-mounted systems with hundreds of litres of oil supplying entire production lines or heavy lifting equipment.

## The main components

### Reservoir (tank)

The reservoir stores the hydraulic fluid. It does more than hold oil: a well-designed tank allows air bubbles to escape, lets contaminants settle, and helps dissipate heat. Baffles separate the return area from the pump suction to give the oil time to settle.

### Electric motor or engine

The prime mover drives the pump. Electric motors are standard for industrial units; diesel or petrol engines are used for mobile and remote applications.

### Hydraulic pump

The pump creates flow. Common types are gear pumps (simple and economical), vane pumps (quiet and efficient at medium pressures) and piston pumps (high pressure and variable displacement for efficient control). Pumps create flow; pressure only rises when that flow meets resistance from a load.

### Pressure relief valve

The relief valve protects the system by limiting maximum pressure. When pressure reaches the set point, it opens and sends flow back to the tank. It is a critical safety component.

### Directional control valves

These valves direct oil to the actuators, controlling whether a cylinder extends, retracts or holds. They can be manually, solenoid or proportionally operated.

### Flow and pressure control valves

Flow control valves set actuator speed. Pressure reducing and sequence valves control pressure in parts of the circuit or the order in which operations happen.

### Filters

Filters remove particles that would otherwise wear pumps, valves and seals. Power packs typically include suction strainers, pressure filters and/or return line filters, often with clogging indicators.

### Cooler

Hydraulic systems generate heat whenever pressure drops without doing useful work. Air-blast or water coolers remove that heat to keep oil within its working temperature range.

### Accessories

Level and temperature gauges, breathers, pressure gauges, accumulators, heaters and electrical control panels complete the unit.

## How it all works together

1. The motor drives the pump, which draws oil from the reservoir.
2. The pump delivers flow into the pressure line.
3. The relief valve limits the maximum pressure.
4. Directional valves send flow to the selected actuator.
5. The actuator moves the load; pressure rises only as much as the load requires.
6. Return oil flows back through the filter and cooler to the reservoir.

## Where hydraulic power packs are used

- Presses, clamping fixtures and machine tools
- Crane and hoist hydraulics
- Material handling and lifting tables
- Test rigs and special-purpose machines
- Mobile equipment and remote sites
- Marine, mining and steel plant equipment

## Standard vs custom power packs

Standard compact units suit simple applications such as a single cylinder with basic control. Custom power packs are designed around specific requirements: flow, pressure, duty cycle, multiple circuits, precise control, environmental conditions and space limits.

A well-designed custom unit is sized for the real duty cycle, which avoids overheating, wasted energy and premature wear.

## Choosing a power pack

When specifying a power pack, prepare the following information:

- required flow and maximum pressure for each actuator;
- duty cycle and number of operations per hour;
- control method: manual, solenoid, proportional or PLC;
- available power supply;
- ambient conditions and installation space;
- noise limits and maintenance access.

Getting the size of a hydraulic power unit right is one of the most important decisions in a hydraulic system. Undersize it and the machine is slow and overheats. Oversize it and you pay for energy, space and equipment you do not need. This guide walks through the main sizing steps.

## Step 1: Define what the actuators must do

Start with the work, not the pump. For each cylinder or hydraulic motor, list:

- the **force** or **torque** required;
- the **speed** required (mm/s or rpm);
- the **stroke** or travel;
- how often it operates (the **duty cycle**);
- which actuators operate at the same time.

## Step 2: Calculate the required pressure

For a cylinder, the required pressure comes from the force and piston area:

**Pressure = Force ÷ Effective area**

Add an allowance for friction, pressure losses through valves and hoses, and a margin for control. A common approach is to design so the working pressure sits comfortably below the maximum rating of the components.

## Step 3: Calculate the required flow

Flow determines speed:

**Flow (L/min) = Effective area (cm²) × Speed (cm/s) × 60 ÷ 1000**

For example, a cylinder with an effective area of 78.5 cm² extending at 5 cm/s needs about 23.6 L/min.

If several actuators move at the same time, add their flows together. If they move in sequence, size for the largest single demand — unless an accumulator is used to supply peak demand.

## Step 4: Calculate motor power

Hydraulic power depends on pressure and flow:

**Power (kW) ≈ Pressure (bar) × Flow (L/min) ÷ 600**

This gives hydraulic power at the pump. Divide by the pump's overall efficiency (often in the region of 0.8–0.9 depending on pump type and condition) to estimate the motor power needed.

For example, 200 bar at 24 L/min is about 8 kW of hydraulic power, so a motor of roughly 9–10 kW would be appropriate before considering duty cycle.

## Step 5: Consider the duty cycle

Many machines need full pressure and flow only for part of the cycle. Pressure-compensated variable pumps, two-stage pumps (high flow at low pressure, low flow at high pressure) or accumulators can reduce motor size and energy consumption significantly.

## Step 6: Size the reservoir

A traditional rule of thumb is a reservoir of around **three times the pump flow per minute** for industrial systems, but modern designs vary. The reservoir must:

- hold enough oil to fill all actuators and lines with margin;
- allow air to separate and contaminants to settle;
- help dissipate heat;
- accommodate thermal expansion.

Mobile and space-limited systems often use smaller tanks combined with better filtration and cooling.

## Step 7: Plan for heat

Every bar of pressure dropped without doing useful work becomes heat. Relief valve flow, throttling and inefficiencies all generate heat. Estimate heat load from the power lost and compare it with what the tank surface can dissipate. If the difference is significant, add a cooler.

## Step 8: Select filtration

Choose filtration based on the most sensitive component in the system. Servo and proportional valves need much finer filtration than simple directional valves. Include clogging indicators so operators know when to change elements.

## Step 9: Choose controls

Decide how the system will be controlled: manual levers, solenoid valves controlled by a PLC, or proportional valves for smooth, variable motion. Controls affect the valve stack, electrical panel and filtration requirements.

## A sizing summary table

| Item | What decides it |
|---|---|
| Pressure | Highest force ÷ smallest effective area, plus losses |
| Flow | Speed × effective area, summed for simultaneous moves |
| Motor power | Pressure × flow ÷ efficiency, adjusted for duty cycle |
| Reservoir | Flow rate, system volume, heat dissipation |
| Cooling | Heat generated vs heat dissipated |
| Filtration | Sensitivity of valves and pumps |

## Get it checked

These calculations give a solid starting point, but every application has details that affect the final design — ambient temperature, noise, space, power supply and maintenance access among them.

Hydraulic components are built with tight internal clearances, often just a few microns. That precision is what makes them efficient — and what makes them vulnerable to contamination. Most hydraulic failures can be traced back to dirty, wet or aerated oil.

This article explains the types of contamination, where they come from, the damage they cause, and practical ways to control them.

## The three main types of contamination

### 1. Solid particles

Dirt, metal wear particles, rubber from hoses and seals, paint flakes and fibres. Particles are the most common contaminant and the most damaging.

### 2. Water

Water enters through condensation, leaking coolers, poor storage and washing equipment. It reduces lubrication, promotes rust, accelerates oil degradation and can cause cavitation damage.

### 3. Air

Air enters through suction leaks, low oil levels, poor return line design and turbulence in the tank. Air causes spongy operation, noise, overheating and oil oxidation.

## Where contamination comes from

- **Built-in contamination:** debris left from manufacturing, assembly or repairs.
- **Ingressed contamination:** dirt entering through breathers, worn wipers, open reservoirs and during maintenance.
- **Generated contamination:** wear particles produced by the system itself, which then cause further wear.
- **New oil:** new oil is not necessarily clean oil. It can contain particles from storage and transfer.

## How contamination damages components

| Contaminant | Damage mechanism | Typical result |
|---|---|---|
| Hard particles | Abrasive wear, silting, erosion | Pump wear, valve sticking, scored cylinders |
| Water | Corrosion, loss of lubrication, additive depletion | Rust, bearing failure, sludge |
| Air | Aeration, cavitation, oxidation | Noise, heat, darkened oil, erratic motion |
| Sludge and varnish | Deposits on valves | Sticking spools, slow response |

Wear particles generated by contamination create more wear in a chain reaction. Once this cycle starts, component life drops rapidly.

## Measuring cleanliness

Oil cleanliness is commonly reported using the **ISO 4406** code, which counts particles above certain sizes in a sample. Each step up in the code represents roughly double the number of particles. Component manufacturers often recommend target cleanliness levels, and more sensitive components such as servo and proportional valves require cleaner oil.

Regular oil analysis can also measure water content, viscosity, wear metals and additive condition — giving early warning before failures happen.

## Practical contamination control

### Keep dirt out

- Fit **desiccant or high-quality breathers** on reservoirs.
- Keep **wipers and rod seals** in good condition.
- **Cap hoses and ports** whenever lines are opened.
- Clean around fittings before disconnecting them.

### Filter new oil

Transfer new oil through a filter cart rather than pouring it directly into the reservoir. Store drums indoors, on their sides, and sealed.

### Remove what gets in

- Use correctly rated **pressure and return filters**.
- Change filter elements based on clogging indicators or oil analysis, not just the calendar.
- Use offline filtration units for large or critical systems.

### Control water and air

- Fix leaking coolers and seals promptly.
- Avoid pressure-washing near breathers and seals.
- Check suction lines for leaks and maintain correct oil levels.
- Make sure return lines discharge below the oil level.

### Clean after repairs

After component replacement or major repairs, **flush the system** to remove debris before returning it to service. Our power packs and repaired cylinders are cleaned and flushed before dispatch for this reason.

## The payoff

Clean oil extends the life of pumps, valves, seals and cylinders and reduces unplanned downtime. For many systems, improving contamination control is one of the lowest-cost reliability improvements available.
