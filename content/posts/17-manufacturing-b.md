Welded joints hold together cylinder end caps, mounting brackets, crane girders and countless other load-bearing components. A poor weld may look acceptable on the surface yet contain defects that lead to cracks and failures under load. For buyers of fabricated components, understanding how weld quality is controlled is essential.

## What determines weld quality?

### Qualified procedures

A **welding procedure specification (WPS)** defines how a weld is made: process, materials, preheat, filler metal, parameters and technique. Procedures are typically qualified by testing sample welds to prove they meet required properties, following recognised welding standards.

### Qualified welders

Welders demonstrate their competence by passing tests for the specific processes, positions and materials they weld.

### Correct materials and filler

Filler metals must match the base material and service requirements. Using the wrong consumable can produce brittle or weak welds.

### Preheat and heat control

Thicker sections and higher-strength steels may need **preheating** to avoid cracking. Controlling heat input also limits distortion and protects material properties.

### Joint preparation and fit-up

Clean, correctly prepared joints with proper fit-up are the foundation of sound welds.

### Post-weld treatment

Some components need **stress relieving** after welding to reduce residual stresses and improve dimensional stability and fatigue performance.

## Common weld defects

| Defect | Description | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Porosity | Gas pores in the weld | Reduced strength, leak paths |
| Lack of fusion | Weld not fused with base metal | Serious weakness |
| Incomplete penetration | Weld not through the joint thickness | Crack initiation, reduced strength |
| Cracks | Hot or cold cracking | Unacceptable; can propagate to failure |
| Undercut | Groove melted into base metal at weld toe | Stress concentration, fatigue |
| Slag inclusions | Trapped slag | Weakness, crack initiation |
| Excessive distortion | Warping from heat | Misalignment, fit problems |

## Inspection methods

- **Visual inspection:** the first and most important check, by trained inspectors.
- **Dye penetrant testing:** reveals surface cracks.
- **Magnetic particle inspection:** detects surface and near-surface cracks in ferromagnetic steels.
- **Ultrasonic testing:** finds internal defects in thicker sections.
- **Radiographic testing:** imaging of internal weld quality in suitable joints.
- **Pressure testing:** for pressure-containing welds such as cylinder end caps and manifold blocks.

The extent of testing should reflect the criticality of the component and any applicable standards.

## Welding in hydraulic components

Hydraulic cylinders contain highly stressed welds, such as the joint between the barrel and end cap and the attachment of ports and mountings. These welds experience pressure cycles and fatigue. Correct joint design, full penetration where required, controlled procedures and pressure testing are essential.

## Questions buyers should ask

1. Are your **welding procedures qualified**, and to which standards?
2. Are your **welders qualified** for this process and material?
3. How do you control **preheat and heat input**?
4. What **inspection and NDT** will be performed, and will I receive reports?
5. Will the component be **pressure tested** (for hydraulic parts)?
6. Is **stress relieving** required, and will it be carried out?
7. How do you ensure **traceability** of materials?

## Red flags

- No documented procedures or welder qualifications.
- Reluctance to provide inspection records.
- Welding on load-bearing parts such as hooks or forged components that should not be welded.
- Cosmetic grinding that hides weld defects.

## Repairs by welding

Welding repairs to load-bearing crane parts and pressure components must be assessed carefully. Some components — such as forged hooks — should never be weld-repaired. Others may be repairable with approved procedures and inspection.

Pressure testing is the final proof that a hydraulic component is fit for service. It verifies that the component holds pressure, does not leak and functions correctly before it is installed. For cylinders, manifolds and power units — new or repaired — a documented pressure test is a key quality checkpoint.

## Why pressure testing matters

- **Detects leaks** from seals, welds, threads and porosity.
- **Confirms structural integrity** of welds and pressure-containing parts.
- **Verifies function**, such as full stroke, smooth movement and cushioning.
- **Provides documented evidence** of quality for customers and audits.

## Common test types

### Proof pressure test

The component is pressurised above its normal working pressure for a defined time, according to the design standard or specification, to confirm it can withstand pressure without permanent deformation or leakage. Test pressures should be defined by the design specification and must never exceed what the component is designed to withstand.

### Internal leakage test

For cylinders, pressure is applied to one side of the piston while the other port is monitored for leakage. This confirms the piston seal is sealing properly.

### External leakage test

Visual inspection of rod seals, static seals, welds and fittings while under pressure.

### Function test

The cylinder or unit is cycled through its full stroke to confirm smooth operation, correct stroke length and cushioning performance. Power units are tested for flow, pressure settings, valve operation and temperature.

### Low-pressure test

Some seal designs can leak at low pressure even if they seal well at high pressure. A low-pressure test checks for this.

## Safety during pressure testing

Pressure testing involves stored energy and must be carried out with strict safety precautions:

- use dedicated test benches with suitable guards and enclosures;
- bleed air from the component before pressurising — compressed air stores far more energy than oil;
- use calibrated gauges and pressure relief protection on the test rig;
- never stand in line with fittings, plugs or end caps under pressure;
- never tighten fittings while under pressure;
- release pressure fully before disconnecting.

## What a good test record contains

| Item | Example |
|---|---|
| Component identification | Part number, serial number, drawing revision |
| Test date and technician | Date, name or ID |
| Test pressure and duration | Specified values |
| Test medium and temperature | Hydraulic oil type, temperature |
| Results | Internal leakage, external leakage, function |
| Gauge identification | Calibrated gauge reference |
| Pass/fail and signature | Inspector sign-off |

Records should be kept and supplied with the component when requested.

## Testing after repair

A repaired cylinder should be tested just like a new one. Testing after repair confirms that seals are correctly installed, that honing and rod repairs are effective and that no damage occurred during assembly.

## Power unit testing

Power packs are typically flushed to remove contamination, then tested for:

- pump flow and pressure;
- relief and pressure valve settings;
- directional valve operation;
- leak-free connections;
- temperature rise during a run test;
- electrical functions and safety devices.

## Ask for the test report

When buying hydraulic components, ask whether testing is standard and request test records. A supplier that tests and documents every unit gives you confidence and traceability.

Engineering drawings are the language of manufacturing. They tell the manufacturer exactly what to make, to what accuracy and from what material. Buyers and maintenance teams who understand the basics can spot missing information, ask better questions and avoid costly mistakes.

## The main parts of a drawing

### Title block

Contains the part name, drawing number, revision, material, scale, units, general tolerances and approval information. Always check that you are using the **latest revision**.

### Views

Orthographic views show the part from different directions. Section views cut through the part to show internal features such as bores and grooves.

### Dimensions

Dimensions define sizes and positions. Critical dimensions usually carry specific tolerances.

### Notes

Notes specify requirements that are not shown graphically: heat treatment, surface treatment, deburring, inspection or testing requirements.

## Understanding tolerances

No part can be made to an exact size. A **tolerance** defines the acceptable range.

- **Bilateral tolerance:** 50.00 ±0.05 means 49.95 to 50.05.
- **Unilateral tolerance:** 50.00 +0.05/-0.00 means 50.00 to 50.05.
- **Limit dimensions:** 49.95–50.05 states the range directly.
- **General tolerances:** apply to dimensions without specific tolerances, usually defined in the title block by reference to a standard.

Tighter tolerances increase manufacturing cost. Good drawings apply tight tolerances only where function requires them.

## Fits between mating parts

Fits describe how two parts go together, such as a shaft in a hole.

| Fit type | Description | Example use |
|---|---|---|
| Clearance fit | Always a gap | Pins that must rotate, sliding parts |
| Transition fit | May have small gap or interference | Locating parts, gears on shafts |
| Interference fit | Always overlap; parts pressed or shrunk together | Bearings on shafts, bushes in housings |

Fits are often specified using the ISO system of limits and fits (for example, H7 for a hole and g6 or k6 for a shaft). The letter indicates the position of the tolerance zone and the number its size.

## Geometric tolerances

Some drawings include **geometric tolerancing** — symbols in boxes specifying requirements such as:

- **straightness** and **flatness**;
- **roundness** and **cylindricity**;
- **concentricity** or **runout** relative to a datum;
- **perpendicularity** and **parallelism**;
- **position** of holes and features.

For hydraulic and rotating parts, runout and concentricity are often critical.

## Surface finish

Surface finish symbols specify roughness, typically as an Ra value. Sealing surfaces, bearing seats and sliding surfaces have specific finish requirements. A missing finish callout on a seal groove or rod surface can lead to early seal failure.

## Common drawing problems when ordering spares

- Outdated revision.
- Missing material or heat treatment information.
- Dimensions taken from worn parts without correction.
- No tolerances on critical features.
- Missing thread specifications.
- Unclear surface finish or coating requirements.

## Before you send a drawing for quotation

1. Check the revision is current.
2. Confirm material and heat treatment are specified.
3. Identify critical dimensions and fits.
4. Add quantity and required documentation (inspection report, material certificate).
5. Include mating part information if the drawing is incomplete.

If you do not have drawings, a manufacturer can create them by reverse engineering, with your approval before production.
