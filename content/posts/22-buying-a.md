A vague request for quotation (RFQ) produces vague quotations — or a long chain of questions that delays your project. A clear RFQ lets suppliers quote accurately and quickly, makes quotations easier to compare, and reduces the risk of receiving a part that does not fit or perform.

Here is a practical structure for RFQs for hydraulic cylinders, power packs and custom engineered components.

## 1. Basic information

- Company name and contact person (with phone and email).
- Project or equipment name.
- Delivery location.
- Required delivery date and whether it is firm.
- Quotation deadline.

## 2. What you need

Describe the item clearly:

- **Type:** cylinder, power pack, manifold, spare part, repair service.
- **Quantity:** initial order and expected annual quantities.
- **New manufacture, repair or both.**
- **Drawings or part numbers,** including revision.
- **Samples or photos** if drawings are not available.

## 3. Technical requirements

### For hydraulic cylinders

| Information | Example |
|---|---|
| Bore / rod / stroke | 100 / 56 / 600 mm |
| Retracted centres | 920 mm |
| Mountings | Clevis both ends, 40 mm pins |
| Ports | 3/4" BSP, orientation as drawing |
| Working / maximum pressure | 200 / 250 bar |
| Fluid | Mineral hydraulic oil |
| Environment | Outdoor, dusty |
| Special requirements | Cushioning on extend, position sensor |

### For power packs

- Actuators and their required forces/speeds.
- Flow and pressure requirements, duty cycle.
- Control method (manual, solenoid, proportional, PLC).
- Power supply (voltage, frequency).
- Reservoir size constraints and installation space.
- Ambient conditions and noise limits.

### For machined or crane parts

- Material and heat treatment.
- Critical dimensions and tolerances.
- Surface finish and coatings.
- Crane or machine details for context.

## 4. Quality and documentation

State the documentation you need:

- dimensional inspection report;
- material certificates;
- hardness test results;
- pressure test records;
- circuit diagrams and manuals;
- compliance with any specified standards.

Requesting documentation at the RFQ stage ensures it is included in the price.

## 5. Commercial requirements

- Packaging and delivery terms.
- Payment terms.
- Warranty expectations.
- Whether partial deliveries are acceptable.
- Spare parts to be quoted at the same time.

## 6. Background information

Explain *why* you need the item:

- Is this a replacement for a failed part? How did it fail?
- Is the equipment being upgraded?
- Is downtime driving urgency?

This context helps suppliers recommend improvements and prioritise urgent work.

## Common RFQ mistakes

- Sending only a part number from an obsolete machine.
- Omitting pressure, load or duty information.
- Forgetting mounting details or port orientations.
- Requesting documentation after the order is placed.
- Comparing quotes that are based on different assumptions.

## Comparing quotations

When quotes arrive, check that each one:

- meets the full specification;
- includes required testing and documentation;
- states lead time realistically;
- clarifies warranty terms;
- highlights any assumptions or exceptions.

The lowest price is not always the lowest cost if it excludes testing or arrives too late.

Hydraulic cylinders are critical to the machines they power. A poorly designed or badly made cylinder leads to leaks, drift, downtime and, in lifting applications, safety risks. Choosing the right manufacturer or repairer matters as much as the specification itself.

Here are the questions worth asking before you place an order.

## 1. Do you design or just assemble?

Some suppliers build only to drawings; others offer engineering support. If your application is new, or the original cylinder failed, you need a supplier who can review loads, buckling, mountings, seals and materials — and suggest improvements.

## 2. Which processes are done in-house?

In-house machining, honing, welding, assembly and testing give better control over quality and lead time. Outsourced processes are not necessarily a problem, but ask how they are controlled.

| Process | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| CNC machining | Accuracy of pistons, glands, grooves and threads |
| Tube honing / finishing | Bore finish and geometry for seal life |
| Rod processing | Hardening, chrome plating and finish quality |
| Welding | Strength and integrity of end caps and mountings |
| Assembly | Clean assembly prevents early contamination |
| Testing | Proves the cylinder before delivery |

## 3. How do you select seals?

A good manufacturer selects seals for the actual pressure, speed, temperature, fluid and environment — not simply the cheapest kit. Ask which seal suppliers they use and whether they can offer upgrades for harsh conditions.

## 4. What testing do you perform?

Every cylinder should be pressure and function tested. Ask what is tested — internal leakage, external leakage, full stroke, cushioning — and whether you will receive a test record.

## 5. What documentation do you provide?

Typical documentation includes drawings, inspection reports, test records and, where needed, material certificates. For repairs, ask for a findings report explaining what was found and why the cylinder failed.

## 6. How do you handle repairs?

Ask whether the repairer:

- inspects and measures all components;
- explains repair options and costs before work;
- replaces all seals, not just the leaking one;
- tests after repair;
- provides a warranty.

## 7. What are your realistic lead times?

Ask for lead times for new cylinders, repairs and urgent breakdown work. A supplier who quotes a realistic date and meets it is more valuable than one who promises a fast date and misses it.

## 8. Can you reverse-engineer obsolete cylinders?

If your equipment is old, you will eventually need cylinders that are no longer available. Ask whether the supplier can measure, draw and manufacture replacements, and whether they will share the approved drawing with you.

## 9. What industries and applications do you serve?

Experience with similar applications — cranes, mining, presses, mobile equipment — means the supplier understands your conditions and common failure modes.

## 10. Can I visit?

A workshop visit tells you a lot: cleanliness, equipment, organisation and the people you will work with.

## Red flags

- No testing, or testing "on request only".
- Vague answers about seal brands or materials.
- No inspection or findings reports.
- Lowest price with no explanation of what is included.
- Reluctance to discuss failures or warranties.

## Build a partnership

The best results come from long-term relationships. A manufacturer who knows your equipment can keep drawings on file, hold critical spares and respond quickly when breakdowns happen.

When a crane part wears out, buyers often face a choice: order from the original equipment manufacturer (OEM) or buy from an aftermarket supplier who manufactures equivalent parts. Both options can be right — depending on the part, the crane and the supplier.

## What are OEM parts?

OEM parts are supplied by the crane's original manufacturer or its authorised suppliers. They are made to the original design specifications.

**Advantages**

- Known fit and specification.
- Direct compatibility with warranty and service agreements.
- Clear part numbers and documentation.

**Limitations**

- Can be expensive.
- Lead times may be long, particularly for older models.
- Parts may be obsolete for older cranes or unavailable if the OEM has ceased trading.

## What are aftermarket parts?

Aftermarket parts are manufactured by independent suppliers, either to OEM drawings supplied by the customer, by reverse engineering, or to equivalent catalogue designs.

**Advantages**

- Often shorter lead times, especially from local manufacturers.
- Cost savings.
- Availability for obsolete equipment.
- Opportunity to improve on the original design or material.

**Limitations**

- Quality varies widely between suppliers.
- Documentation may be limited unless requested.
- May affect warranty terms on newer cranes.

## Comparison

| Factor | OEM | Quality aftermarket |
|---|---|---|
| Fit | Assured | Assured when made to drawing or carefully reverse-engineered |
| Lead time | Variable, often long for older cranes | Often shorter |
| Cost | Higher | Often lower |
| Availability for old cranes | Limited | Good |
| Improvements | Rare | Possible |
| Documentation | Standard | Request inspection reports and certificates |

## Which parts suit aftermarket supply?

Many mechanical parts are well suited to quality aftermarket manufacture:

- wheels, axles and bearing housings;
- pins, bushes and shafts;
- sheaves and drums;
- couplings and brake drums;
- fabricated brackets and guards.

For proprietary electronic systems, safety devices and certified components such as hooks and rated lifting accessories, OEM or certified equivalents are usually the appropriate choice.

## How to evaluate an aftermarket supplier

1. **Engineering capability:** can they measure, draw and specify materials and heat treatment correctly?
2. **Manufacturing control:** in-house machining, heat treatment control and inspection.
3. **Documentation:** dimensional reports, hardness results and material certificates.
4. **Experience:** familiarity with cranes and their duty classes.
5. **Drawing approval:** do they provide drawings for your approval and records?
6. **References:** other crane users they supply.

## Warranty and compliance considerations

For cranes under warranty or service contracts, check whether non-OEM parts are permitted. For any load-bearing component, ensure the replacement meets or exceeds the original specification and that significant changes are reviewed by qualified engineers.

## A balanced strategy

Many maintenance teams use both: OEM parts for proprietary electronics and newer cranes, and quality aftermarket parts for mechanical wear components and obsolete equipment. Keeping approved drawings for aftermarket parts builds independence and reduces future lead times.
