The hook and load block carry every load the crane lifts. They are subject to wear from slings, impact from loads and fatigue from repeated lifting. Because a hook failure can drop a load, hook and block inspection is a core part of every crane inspection routine.

This guide covers the basics. Always follow the hook manufacturer's criteria, applicable standards and your local regulations, and ensure inspections are performed by trained, competent personnel.

## Parts of a hook and load block

- **Hook:** the forged hook body, including the saddle (bowl), throat, tip and shank.
- **Safety latch:** closes the hook throat to prevent slings slipping off.
- **Hook nut and thrust bearing:** secure the hook and allow rotation.
- **Block frame and side plates:** hold the sheaves and hook assembly.
- **Sheaves:** guide the wire rope.
- **Swivel:** allows the hook to rotate under load (on many blocks).

## What to inspect on the hook

### Throat opening

An increase in throat opening indicates the hook has been overloaded or bent. Many inspection programmes measure the throat opening against a reference value recorded when the hook was new, or between marks punched on the hook. A significant increase means the hook must be removed from service.

### Wear in the saddle

Slings wear the bowl of the hook over time. Wear beyond the manufacturer's limit reduces strength.

### Twisting

A hook twisted from its original plane has been overloaded or side-loaded and must be removed from service.

### Cracks, nicks and gouges

Look for cracks, especially at the saddle and shank. Nicks and gouges create stress concentrations. Non-destructive testing, such as magnetic particle inspection, may be required during periodic examinations.

### Latch

The safety latch must be present, undamaged and close the throat properly. A missing or damaged latch should be replaced before use.

### Shank and threads

Check the shank for bending and the threaded area for corrosion and damage. The hook nut and its locking device must be secure.

## What to inspect on the load block

- **Sheaves:** groove wear, flange damage and free rotation.
- **Side plates:** cracks, bending and corrosion.
- **Fasteners:** tightness and locking devices.
- **Swivel and thrust bearing:** smooth rotation without roughness or excessive play.
- **Rope guards:** present and correctly positioned to keep the rope in the sheave grooves.
- **Capacity marking:** clearly legible.

## Remove from service if…

| Condition | Action |
|---|---|
| Throat opening increased beyond limit | Remove from service |
| Hook twisted from its plane | Remove from service |
| Cracks found | Remove from service |
| Saddle wear beyond manufacturer's limit | Remove from service |
| Latch missing or not working | Repair before use |
| Rough or seized swivel | Repair before use |

**Never weld, heat, straighten or grind a damaged hook.** Heating and welding can destroy the material properties of a forged hook. Damaged hooks must be replaced with correctly rated parts.

## Inspection frequency

- **Pre-use:** visual check by the operator for obvious damage and latch function.
- **Frequent inspections:** more detailed checks at intervals based on usage.
- **Periodic thorough examination:** by a competent person, including measurements and, where required, NDT.

## Good lifting practice protects hooks

- Never side-load or tip-load a hook.
- Seat slings in the saddle of the hook, not on the tip.
- Never exceed the rated capacity.
- Avoid shock loading.
- Use suitable shackles or master links for multiple slings.

When a production crane breaks down, the cost goes far beyond the repair. Production stops, deliveries slip and people stand idle. Yet most crane failures are preceded by warning signs. A planned maintenance approach catches those signs early and turns emergencies into scheduled work.

## Step 1: Rank your cranes by criticality

Not every crane deserves the same attention. Rank each crane by:

- **impact on production** if it stops;
- **availability of alternatives** (another crane or forklift);
- **safety consequences** of failure;
- **utilisation** and duty.

Focus your maintenance effort and spares budget on the cranes that matter most.

## Step 2: Move from reactive to planned work

| Approach | Description | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Reactive | Fix when it breaks | Unplanned downtime, high cost |
| Preventive | Fixed-interval inspections and replacements | Fewer failures, some unnecessary work |
| Condition-based | Act on measured condition | Work done when needed |
| Proactive | Remove root causes | Fewer failures over time |

Most sites benefit from a combination: preventive inspections for safety-critical items, condition monitoring for expensive components, and root cause analysis for repeat failures.

## Step 3: Monitor condition

Simple measurements reveal developing problems:

- **wheel and rail wear** measurements over time;
- **brake lining thickness** and air gap;
- **gearbox oil analysis** for wear metals;
- **thermography** of motors, panels and gearboxes;
- **vibration checks** on high-duty drives;
- **wire rope** inspection records.

Trends matter more than single readings. A wheel that loses flange thickness twice as fast as last year indicates an alignment problem.

## Step 4: Plan spares

The fastest repair is useless if the part takes six weeks to arrive. For each critical crane:

- identify parts with long lead times;
- hold critical spares on site;
- reverse-engineer obsolete parts before they fail;
- keep approved drawings for manufactured parts.

## Step 5: Train operators

Operators are the first line of defence. Good practices include:

- thorough pre-use checks and prompt defect reporting;
- smooth operation without excessive inching or plugging;
- avoiding side pulls and shock loading;
- respecting rated capacities.

Poor operating practice accelerates wear on brakes, wheels, ropes and structures.

## Step 6: Investigate repeat failures

If the same part fails repeatedly, replacing it again is not the solution. Ask:

- Is the part correct for the crane's duty?
- Is there misalignment, overload or contamination?
- Has the crane's usage changed?
- Could a better material or design last longer?

Document findings and implement corrective action.

## Step 7: Schedule work around production

Plan major work — wheel replacements, rope changes, gearbox overhauls — during planned shutdowns. With parts ready and procedures prepared, jobs that might take days in an emergency can be completed in hours.

## Step 8: Keep good records

A maintenance management system or even a well-kept spreadsheet should record inspections, findings, repairs, parts used and downtime for each crane. Records reveal which cranes and components cause most downtime and where investment will pay back fastest.

## A simple improvement plan

1. List cranes and rank criticality.
2. Review the last year's breakdowns and their causes.
3. Build a critical spares list and fill the gaps.
4. Introduce condition checks on wheels, brakes and gearboxes.
5. Review results quarterly and adjust.

Overhead (EOT) cranes and gantry cranes both lift and move loads using a hoist and trolley. The difference is how the crane is supported. That difference affects the structure, the travel system, the working environment — and the maintenance each crane needs.

## The basic difference

**Overhead cranes** run on rails mounted on runway beams, usually supported by the building structure or dedicated columns. The bridge spans the bay above the working area.

**Gantry cranes** have legs that run on rails at ground level (or on wheels or tyres). They are used outdoors, in yards, ports and areas where a building structure is not available. **Semi-gantry cranes** combine one gantry leg with a runway on the other side.

## Component comparison

| Area | Overhead crane | Gantry crane |
|---|---|---|
| Support | Runway beams on building or columns | Legs on ground rails or tyres |
| Travel wheels | At bridge ends, on elevated rails | At leg bases, on ground rails |
| Environment | Usually indoors | Often outdoors |
| Exposure | Dust, heat in some industries | Weather, wind, corrosion, debris |
| Power supply | Conductor bars or festoon | Cable reels, festoons, conductor bars |
| Typical issues | Runway alignment, skewing | Rail settlement, wind effects, corrosion |

## Maintenance differences

### Travel system

On overhead cranes, runway alignment depends on the building structure. Settlement, thermal movement and damage can misalign rails, leading to wheel flange wear and skewing.

On gantry cranes, ground rails can settle or shift with foundations. Debris on rails, corrosion and heavy traffic crossings add wear. Rail sweepers and guards are particularly important on gantry cranes.

### Environment and corrosion

Outdoor gantry cranes face rain, sun, salt and temperature swings. Maintenance plans should include:

- corrosion inspection and protective coating repairs;
- drainage of water traps in the structure;
- weatherproofing of electrical enclosures;
- lubricants suitable for outdoor temperatures.

### Wind

Outdoor cranes must be protected against wind. Storm brakes, rail clamps, anchors and wind speed alarms are essential safety systems. Inspect and test them regularly, and train operators to secure the crane when wind limits are approached.

### Power supply

Cable reels and festoon systems on gantry cranes are exposed to weather and mechanical damage. Check cables, slip rings and reel mechanisms regularly.

### Structure

Gantry crane legs and connections carry varying loads as the trolley moves. Periodic structural inspection for cracks and corrosion, especially at welded connections, is essential.

## Shared maintenance areas

Both crane types share hoists, trolleys, brakes, ropes, hooks and controls. The same inspection principles apply: wire rope and sheave checks, brake maintenance, limit switch testing and hook inspection.

## Spare parts considerations

Outdoor gantry cranes may need:

- wheels with tougher, more wear-resistant treads;
- sealed bearings to keep out water and dust;
- corrosion-resistant fasteners and coatings;
- spare cable reel components.

Port gantry cranes and container cranes, which operate continuously, typically fall into higher duty classes and benefit from critical spares held on site.
