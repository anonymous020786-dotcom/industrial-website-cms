A hydraulic motor is the rotary counterpart of a hydraulic cylinder. Instead of pushing in a straight line, it converts hydraulic flow and pressure into rotation and torque. Hydraulic motors drive winches, slew rings, conveyors, mixers, wheels and tracks — anywhere high torque is needed from a compact unit.

## How a hydraulic motor works

Pressurised oil acts on internal elements — gears, vanes or pistons — to create rotation. Two simple relationships describe its performance:

- **Speed depends on flow:** the more oil supplied, the faster the motor turns.
- **Torque depends on pressure and displacement:** higher pressure or larger displacement gives more torque.

Displacement is the volume of oil required for one revolution. A large-displacement motor turns more slowly for a given flow but produces more torque.

## Main types of hydraulic motor

### Gear motors

Simple, compact and economical. Suitable for moderate pressures and speeds, such as fans, conveyors and auxiliary drives. Efficiency is lower at low speeds.

### Vane motors

Smooth and relatively quiet, with good performance at medium speeds. Used in industrial applications such as machine tools and conveyors.

### Axial piston motors

High efficiency and high pressure capability. Available in fixed and variable displacement. Used where efficiency and power density matter, including mobile propulsion and industrial drives.

### Low-speed high-torque (LSHT) motors

These include **radial piston** and **orbital (gerotor/geroler)** motors. They produce very high torque at low speeds, often eliminating the need for a gearbox. Typical uses include winches, slewing drives, augers, wheel drives and conveyors.

## Comparison overview

| Type | Speed range | Torque | Efficiency | Typical uses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gear | Medium–high | Low–medium | Moderate | Fans, auxiliary drives |
| Vane | Medium | Medium | Good | Industrial drives |
| Axial piston | Wide | Medium–high | Very good | Propulsion, high-power drives |
| Radial piston (LSHT) | Low | Very high | Very good | Winches, heavy drives |
| Orbital (LSHT) | Low–medium | High | Moderate–good | Augers, conveyors, light winches |

## Selection criteria

### 1. Torque requirement

Calculate the torque needed to start and run the load, including breakaway torque, acceleration and any shock loads. Starting torque is often significantly higher than running torque.

### 2. Speed range

Determine minimum and maximum speeds. Some motors run smoothly at very low speeds; others become jerky or inefficient.

### 3. Pressure and flow available

Match motor displacement to the available pressure and flow from the power unit.

### 4. Load type

Consider whether the motor must hold a load, reverse frequently, or experience overrunning loads (for example, lowering on a winch). Overrunning loads require counterbalance or brake valves, and holding loads usually need a mechanical brake because hydraulic motors leak internally.

### 5. Shaft and mounting

Check shaft type, side load capacity, mounting flange and whether the motor will support external radial loads from pulleys or sprockets.

### 6. Case drain and back pressure

Many motors require a case drain line. Excess back pressure on the return or case can damage shaft seals.

## Braking and load holding

Because hydraulic motors have internal leakage, they should not be relied on to hold a suspended load. Winches and hoists use spring-applied, hydraulically released brakes and counterbalance valves to hold and control loads safely.

## Signs of motor wear

- Reduced speed under load.
- Increased case drain flow.
- Overheating.
- Shaft seal leaks.
- Noise and vibration.

When a hydraulic pump wears out, replacement is not the only option. Many pumps — particularly larger piston pumps — can be reconditioned to perform like new for a lower cost and often with a shorter lead time. But the quality of repair varies widely. Knowing what a proper repair involves helps you choose a repairer and evaluate quotations.

## The repair process

### 1. Initial assessment

The repairer records the pump's details: make, model, part number, displacement, rotation and controls. They note any external damage and, ideally, ask about the failure symptoms and operating conditions.

### 2. Strip and clean

The pump is fully dismantled. Every part is cleaned so that wear and damage can be properly inspected.

### 3. Inspection and measurement

Critical components are measured against tolerances. For piston pumps this includes pistons and bores, slippers, swash plate, valve plate, cylinder block, bearings and shaft. Gear pumps are checked for wear on gears, bushings and the housing.

### 4. Root cause findings

A good repairer explains *why* the pump failed. Common findings include:

- **Abrasive wear** — contamination.
- **Erosion pitting** — cavitation or aeration.
- **Scored or burnt surfaces** — lack of lubrication, overheating or dry start.
- **Broken parts** — pressure spikes or mechanical overload.

### 5. Quotation

You should receive a quote listing the parts to be replaced or remanufactured, with an option to compare the cost against a new pump.

### 6. Rebuild

Worn parts are replaced or remanufactured. Precision surfaces such as valve plates can sometimes be lapped. All seals, bearings and wear items are replaced.

### 7. Performance testing

This step separates professional repairs from basic rebuilds. The pump is run on a test bench to check flow at various pressures, control functions (such as compensator settings), case drain leakage and noise. A test report documents the results.

## Repair or replace?

| Consider repair when… | Consider replacement when… |
|---|---|
| The housing and major parts are reusable | The housing or rotating group is badly damaged |
| The pump is large or expensive | A new pump costs little more than repair |
| New lead times are long | The model is obsolete with no parts available |
| The application needs an exact match | You want to upgrade to a more efficient design |

## Questions to ask your repairer

1. Will the pump be **performance tested**, and will I receive a test report?
2. What **parts** will be replaced, and are they genuine or equivalent quality?
3. Will you tell me the **cause of failure**?
4. What **warranty** applies?
5. What is the **turnaround time**?

## After the repair: protect your investment

A rebuilt pump will fail again if the underlying cause is not fixed. Before fitting it:

- clean or flush the system to remove debris from the original failure;
- replace filter elements;
- correct suction-side problems if cavitation was found;
- fill the pump case with clean oil before starting;
- follow the correct start-up procedure.

## Exchange units for critical equipment

For critical machines, consider keeping a reconditioned exchange pump on the shelf. When a pump fails, fit the spare immediately and send the failed pump for repair.

You do not need complex software to make sensible decisions about hydraulic systems. A handful of formulas covers most everyday questions: how much flow a pump delivers, how much power it needs, how fast a cylinder will move and how much torque a motor will produce.

The formulas below use common metric units. Results are theoretical; real systems include losses, so always allow a margin.

## 1. Pump flow

**Flow (L/min) = Displacement (cc/rev) × Speed (rpm) ÷ 1000**

**Example:** a pump with 20 cc/rev displacement driven at 1,450 rpm delivers:

20 × 1,450 ÷ 1000 = **29 L/min** (theoretical)

Actual flow is lower due to internal leakage. Volumetric efficiency depends on pump type, condition, pressure and viscosity.

## 2. Hydraulic power

**Power (kW) = Pressure (bar) × Flow (L/min) ÷ 600**

**Example:** 29 L/min at 180 bar:

180 × 29 ÷ 600 = **8.7 kW** hydraulic power

## 3. Drive motor power

**Motor power (kW) = Hydraulic power ÷ Overall pump efficiency**

With an overall efficiency of around 0.85:

8.7 ÷ 0.85 ≈ **10.2 kW**

You would typically select the next standard motor size, considering duty cycle and starting conditions.

## 4. Pump torque

**Torque (Nm) ≈ Displacement (cc/rev) × Pressure (bar) ÷ 62.8**

**Example:** 20 cc/rev at 180 bar:

20 × 180 ÷ 62.8 ≈ **57 Nm** (theoretical, before mechanical losses)

This is the torque the drive motor or engine must supply.

## 5. Cylinder area and force

**Area (cm²) = π × Diameter (cm)² ÷ 4**

**Force (kN) = Pressure (bar) × Area (cm²) ÷ 100**

**Example:** an 80 mm bore cylinder has an area of about 50.3 cm². At 180 bar:

180 × 50.3 ÷ 100 ≈ **90.5 kN** (about 9.2 tonnes-force)

## 6. Cylinder speed

**Speed (m/s) = Flow (L/min) ÷ (Area (cm²) × 6)**

**Example:** 29 L/min into the 80 mm cylinder:

29 ÷ (50.3 × 6) ≈ **0.096 m/s**, or about 96 mm/s

## 7. Hydraulic motor speed and torque

**Motor speed (rpm) = Flow (L/min) × 1000 ÷ Displacement (cc/rev)**

**Motor torque (Nm) ≈ Displacement (cc/rev) × Pressure (bar) ÷ 62.8**

**Example:** a 100 cc/rev motor supplied with 29 L/min at 180 bar:

- Speed = 29 × 1000 ÷ 100 = **290 rpm**
- Torque ≈ 100 × 180 ÷ 62.8 ≈ **287 Nm** (before efficiency losses)

## 8. Heat generated

Any power not converted into useful work becomes heat. If 3 kW is lost through a relief valve and throttling, the system must dissipate 3 kW of heat — through the reservoir and a cooler if needed.

## Quick reference table

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Pump flow (L/min) | cc/rev × rpm ÷ 1000 |
| Hydraulic power (kW) | bar × L/min ÷ 600 |
| Motor power (kW) | Hydraulic kW ÷ efficiency |
| Torque (Nm) | cc/rev × bar ÷ 62.8 |
| Cylinder force (kN) | bar × cm² ÷ 100 |
| Cylinder speed (m/s) | L/min ÷ (cm² × 6) |
| Motor speed (rpm) | L/min × 1000 ÷ cc/rev |

## Use the numbers wisely

These calculations are excellent for sanity checks, comparing options and preparing enquiries. Final designs should account for efficiency, pressure losses, duty cycle, temperature and safety factors.
