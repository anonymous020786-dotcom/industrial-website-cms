Hydraulic components depend on precision. A piston must fit its bore with a controlled clearance, a gland must locate a seal in exactly the right groove, and a manifold must route oil through intersecting drillings without leaks. Small dimensional errors lead to leakage, seal failure and lost performance. This is why CNC machining is central to modern hydraulic manufacturing.

## What CNC machining brings

Computer numerical control (CNC) machines follow programmed toolpaths with high accuracy. For hydraulic components, this provides:

- **Accuracy:** dimensions and geometry held within tight tolerances.
- **Repeatability:** the hundredth part matches the first.
- **Complex geometry:** multi-axis machining of grooves, ports and contours.
- **Surface finish control:** consistent finishes for sealing surfaces.
- **Efficiency:** shorter cycle times for batch and repeat work.

## Typical CNC-machined hydraulic parts

| Component | Key machined features |
|---|---|
| Pistons | Seal and wear-ring grooves, bore for rod, concentricity |
| Glands | Rod seal, wiper and O-ring grooves, threads |
| Piston rods | Threads, shoulders, mounting features before plating |
| End caps and clevises | Bores, pin holes, ports, threads |
| Manifolds | Valve cavities, cross-drillings, port threads |
| Shafts and couplings | Keyways, splines, bearing seats |

## Why tolerances matter

### Seal grooves

Seal manufacturers specify groove dimensions and tolerances. A groove that is too deep allows the seal to move and leak; too shallow and the seal is over-compressed, increasing friction and wear.

### Concentricity

Pistons, glands and rods must be concentric. Eccentricity loads seals unevenly and causes uneven wear.

### Clearances

Clearance gaps behind seals affect extrusion resistance at high pressure. Too large a gap lets the seal extrude and fail.

### Threads

Gland threads and port threads must be accurate and clean to seal correctly and carry load.

## Surface finish

Sealing surfaces need specific finishes. Too rough and seals wear; too smooth and lubrication suffers. CNC machining combined with appropriate finishing processes — grinding, honing, polishing or burnishing — produces the right finish for each surface.

## Deburring and cleanliness

Sharp edges and burrs cut seals during assembly and can break off into the oil as contamination. Thorough deburring and edge-breaking, followed by cleaning, are essential steps in hydraulic manufacturing.

## Inspection

Machining is only as good as its verification. Typical inspection methods include:

- calipers, micrometers and bore gauges for critical dimensions;
- thread gauges for ports and gland threads;
- surface roughness measurement on sealing surfaces;
- first-article inspection for new parts and periodic checks during batches.

Inspection results should be recorded, especially for safety-critical and customer-specified dimensions.

## CNC and reverse engineering

CNC machining makes reverse engineering practical. Once a worn part is measured and a drawing approved, CNC programs reproduce the component accurately — and the program can be reused for future orders, guaranteeing consistency.

## Choosing a machining partner

Look for:

- appropriate machine capacity for your part sizes;
- understanding of hydraulic sealing requirements;
- documented inspection processes;
- the ability to combine machining with heat treatment, plating and assembly.

The bore of a hydraulic cylinder barrel is where the piston seals do their work. Its diameter, straightness, roundness and surface finish all affect how well the cylinder seals and how long the seals last. Honing is the finishing process that gives the bore these properties.

## What is honing?

Honing uses abrasive stones mounted on a rotating, reciprocating tool. As the tool moves through the bore, the stones remove small amounts of material, correcting geometry and producing a controlled surface texture with a characteristic **cross-hatch pattern**.

Many cylinder tubes are supplied pre-finished — for example, **skived and roller-burnished** tubes — which achieve excellent finishes efficiently. Honing is used both in manufacturing and in repair to correct and refinish bores.

## Why bore finish matters

### Seal life

A bore that is too rough acts like an abrasive, wearing seals quickly. A bore that is too smooth may not hold an adequate lubricating film, causing friction, heat and stick-slip.

### Leakage

Scratches and scoring along the bore create leak paths past the piston seal, causing drift and loss of force.

### Wear rings and guidance

Accurate bore geometry keeps the piston centred and wear rings evenly loaded.

## Geometry as well as finish

Honing corrects:

- **diameter** to the specified tolerance;
- **roundness** (ovality);
- **straightness** and taper along the length.

These geometric properties matter as much as surface roughness. An out-of-round bore cannot be sealed properly, regardless of finish.

## Typical bore defects in repair work

| Defect | Common cause |
|---|---|
| Longitudinal scoring | Contaminated oil, failed wear rings |
| Localised pitting | Water in oil, corrosion during storage |
| Bell-mouthing at ends | Side loads, wear at stroke ends |
| Barrel bulging | Overpressure, shock loads |
| Ovality | Side loads, clamping damage, thin walls |

## Can a worn tube be re-honed?

Re-honing can restore a bore with light scoring or minor corrosion. Each hone removes material, increasing the bore diameter. The questions are:

1. Will the bore remain within the tolerance required by the piston seal?
2. Will the wall thickness remain sufficient for the working pressure?
3. Can defects be fully removed without excessive enlargement?

If the bore would become oversize, options include fitting an oversize piston and seals (where appropriate and safe) or replacing the tube.

Bulged barrels should not be honed back into service. Bulging indicates the barrel has been overstressed and should be replaced.

## Inspection after honing

- Bore diameter measured at several points along the length and around the circumference.
- Surface roughness measurement.
- Visual inspection under good lighting for remaining defects.
- Cleaning to remove all abrasive residue before assembly — honing grit left in a cylinder is severe contamination.

## Preventing bore damage

- Keep oil clean with effective filtration.
- Maintain wear rings so pistons do not contact the bore.
- Remove water from oil and store cylinders properly.
- Avoid side loads through good alignment and mounting design.

Many heavy engineering components need two conflicting properties: a surface hard enough to resist wear and a core tough enough to absorb shocks without cracking. Induction hardening delivers both. It is widely used on piston rods, pins, shafts, gears and crane wheels.

## How induction hardening works

1. **Heating:** an alternating current passes through a copper coil (inductor) around or near the part. This induces electrical currents in the surface of the steel, heating it rapidly above its transformation temperature.
2. **Quenching:** the heated surface is immediately cooled, usually with a water-based quenchant, forming a hard martensitic layer.
3. **Tempering:** the part is often tempered at a lower temperature to relieve stresses and adjust hardness and toughness.

Because heating is concentrated at the surface, the core remains relatively unaffected and keeps its toughness.

## Key benefits

- **High surface hardness** for wear resistance.
- **Tough core** for impact and fatigue resistance.
- **Improved fatigue strength** due to beneficial compressive stresses in the surface layer.
- **Selective hardening:** only the areas that need it are hardened.
- **Speed and repeatability** for production work.
- **Less distortion** than through-hardening large parts.

## Typical applications

| Component | Why induction hardening is used |
|---|---|
| Piston rods | Hard base under chrome resists dents and wear |
| Pins and bushes | Wear resistance at bearing surfaces |
| Shafts | Wear resistance at bearing and seal seats, fatigue strength |
| Gears | Hard, wear-resistant teeth with tough cores |
| Crane wheels | Hard treads and flanges with tough wheel bodies |

## What to specify

When ordering induction-hardened components, specify:

- **Surface hardness** range.
- **Effective case depth:** the depth at which hardness drops to a specified value. Case depth must suit the loads and allow for wear and any finish grinding.
- **Hardened zones:** which areas must be hardened and which must remain soft (for example, threads or areas to be welded).
- **Material grade:** the steel must contain sufficient carbon and alloying elements to respond to induction hardening.

## Material selection matters

Induction hardening works best on medium-carbon and suitable alloy steels. Low-carbon steels do not harden significantly. The material must also be clean and free of defects to avoid cracking during quenching.

## Quality control

- **Hardness testing** on the surface and, where required, on sections or test pieces to confirm case depth.
- **Crack detection** using magnetic particle inspection for critical parts.
- **Dimensional checks** after heat treatment and finishing.

## Common problems and causes

| Problem | Possible cause |
|---|---|
| Low surface hardness | Wrong material, insufficient heating, poor quench |
| Cracking | Excessive hardness, sharp corners, material defects |
| Insufficient case depth | Process parameters, coil design |
| Distortion | Uneven heating or quenching |

Good design helps: generous radii instead of sharp corners, uniform sections and avoiding holes near hardened zones reduce cracking risk.

## Induction hardening in repair and reverse engineering

When reverse-engineering worn parts, hardness testing often shows that the original part was surface-hardened. Specifying the correct hardening in the replacement is essential for achieving the original — or better — service life.
