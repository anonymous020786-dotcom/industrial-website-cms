When comparing quotations for hydraulic equipment, it is natural to focus on the purchase price. But for most hydraulic systems, the purchase price is a small part of the total cost over the equipment's life. Energy, maintenance, repairs and downtime usually cost far more. **Total cost of ownership (TCO)** puts all these costs on the table.

## The components of TCO

### 1. Purchase and installation

The initial price of the equipment, plus installation, commissioning and any modifications to fit it.

### 2. Energy

Hydraulic power units can consume significant electricity, especially on machines that run many hours. Inefficient pumps, throttling losses and heat generation all add up.

### 3. Maintenance

Filters, oil, seals, inspections and labour over the life of the equipment.

### 4. Repairs and spare parts

The cost of repairing failures, including parts and labour.

### 5. Downtime

Lost production while equipment is broken or under maintenance. For critical equipment, downtime is often the largest cost of all.

### 6. End of life

Disposal, replacement or refurbishment at the end of service life.

## An illustrative comparison

Consider two power units for the same machine, operating many hours a year:

| Cost element | Option A (low price) | Option B (efficient design) |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase price | Lower | Higher |
| Pump type | Fixed displacement | Variable displacement |
| Energy use | Higher (excess flow to heat) | Lower (flow matched to demand) |
| Cooling | Larger cooler needed | Smaller cooler |
| Oil and seal life | Shorter (higher temperatures) | Longer |
| Downtime risk | Higher | Lower |

On machines with high utilisation, the energy and reliability savings of the more efficient design can exceed the difference in purchase price over the equipment's life. The exact figures depend on operating hours, electricity prices and duty cycle — which is why a TCO comparison should use your own data.

## Where TCO decisions matter most

- **Power units** on machines running multiple shifts.
- **Pumps** where variable displacement or variable speed drives reduce energy use.
- **Cylinders** on critical equipment, where reliability prevents downtime.
- **Filtration**, where better contamination control extends component life.
- **Crane wheels and wear parts**, where better materials reduce replacement frequency.

## How to estimate TCO

1. **Estimate operating hours** per year and equipment life.
2. **Estimate energy use** from motor power and duty cycle.
3. **List maintenance items** and their frequencies.
4. **Estimate failure frequency** and repair costs based on experience.
5. **Value downtime** per hour for the affected process.
6. **Compare options** over the same period.

Even rough estimates often reveal that higher-quality options pay back.

## Ways to reduce TCO

- Choose efficient pump and control strategies.
- Specify appropriate filtration and cooling.
- Select durable materials and seals for the actual conditions.
- Standardise components to simplify spares.
- Plan maintenance based on condition monitoring.
- Hold critical spares to reduce downtime.

## Ask suppliers for TCO information

Suppliers can often provide information on energy consumption, expected service intervals and component life. Asking for this information encourages quotations based on value rather than lowest price.

"When can you deliver?" is often the first question buyers ask. For custom hydraulic and crane components, the answer depends on several factors — some controlled by the manufacturer, others by the buyer. Understanding them helps you plan better and shorten lead times when it matters.

## What drives lead time

### 1. Information completeness

Missing information is the most common cause of delay. Every question that needs answering before design or manufacturing can start adds days.

### 2. Engineering and drawings

New designs and reverse-engineered parts need engineering time for measurements, drawings and calculations. Repeat orders with approved drawings skip this step.

### 3. Customer approval

Drawings and specifications usually need customer approval. Approval delays add directly to lead time.

### 4. Materials

Standard steel grades and sizes are usually available quickly. Special alloys, large forgings, specific tube sizes and chrome bars may need to be ordered, adding significant time.

### 5. Bought-in components

Seals, pumps, valves, motors, sensors and bearings come from external suppliers. Their lead times can dominate the overall schedule.

### 6. Processes

Heat treatment, plating, coating and specialised finishing often involve external processors or batch scheduling.

### 7. Testing and documentation

Pressure testing, inspection and documentation add time but reduce the risk of problems after delivery.

### 8. Workshop capacity

Current workload affects when work can start.

## Typical timeline structure

| Stage | Factors |
|---|---|
| Quotation | Completeness of enquiry |
| Engineering and approval | Design complexity, approval speed |
| Procurement | Materials and bought-in components |
| Manufacture | Machining, welding, heat treatment, coatings |
| Assembly and testing | Complexity, test requirements |
| Dispatch | Packaging, documentation, transport |

## How buyers can shorten lead times

1. **Provide complete information** at the enquiry stage: drawings, samples, specifications and documentation requirements.
2. **Approve drawings quickly** and nominate a decision-maker.
3. **Keep approved drawings on file** for future repeat orders.
4. **Order long-lead items early**, such as pumps and special materials, even before final details are fixed.
5. **Accept equivalent standard components** where the original is hard to obtain.
6. **Plan replacements** using condition monitoring rather than waiting for failure.
7. **Hold critical spares** for components with long lead times.

## Handling breakdowns

When a breakdown occurs, ask the manufacturer about:

- repairing the existing part rather than making a new one;
- temporary solutions to restore operation;
- prioritised manufacturing for critical components;
- partial deliveries — for example, one urgent unit, with spares following later.

Clear communication about the cost of downtime helps suppliers prioritise effectively.

## Realistic promises

Be wary of unrealistically short lead times. A supplier who commits to an achievable date and keeps you informed is more valuable than one who promises a fast delivery and misses it.

A component can look perfect and still be wrong. Documentation provides evidence that dimensions, materials, heat treatment and performance meet requirements. It also creates a record that helps future maintenance, repairs and audits. Knowing what to request — and requesting it before the order — is part of good purchasing.

## Common types of documentation

### Dimensional inspection report

Records measured dimensions against drawing requirements, typically for critical dimensions and tolerances. Essential for machined parts, wheels, pins, shafts and reverse-engineered components.

### Material test certificate

Issued by the steel mill or supplier, documenting chemical composition and mechanical properties of the material. Important for load-bearing and safety-critical components.

### Hardness test report

Confirms the results of heat treatment. For surface-hardened parts such as crane wheels and induction-hardened rods, the report may include surface hardness and, where specified, hardened depth.

### Pressure and function test record

For hydraulic cylinders, manifolds and power units, documents test pressures, durations, leakage results and function checks.

### Welding records

For critical fabrications: welding procedures, welder qualifications and NDT reports.

### NDT reports

Results of non-destructive testing such as magnetic particle, dye penetrant or ultrasonic inspection.

### Coating and plating reports

Thickness and adhesion results for paints, plating or chrome where specified.

### Drawings, circuit diagrams and manuals

As-built drawings, hydraulic circuit diagrams, parts lists and operating and maintenance instructions — particularly for custom systems.

## What to request for different products

| Product | Typical documentation |
|---|---|
| Hydraulic cylinder (new) | Drawing, test record, inspection of key dimensions |
| Hydraulic cylinder (repair) | Findings report, parts replaced, test record |
| Power pack | Circuit diagram, parts list, test record, manual |
| Crane wheels | Dimensional report, hardness report, material certificate |
| Pins and shafts | Dimensional report, material certificate, hardness report if treated |
| Fabricated structures | Dimensional report, welding and NDT records if specified |

## Why documentation matters

- **Quality assurance:** confirms requirements were met.
- **Traceability:** links components to materials and processes.
- **Maintenance:** provides baseline data for future inspections.
- **Root cause analysis:** helps investigate failures.
- **Compliance:** supports audits and regulatory requirements.
- **Future orders:** documentation and drawings make repeat orders faster and more consistent.

## Request it at the start

Documentation takes time to produce and may require specific inspection steps during manufacturing. Include documentation requirements in your RFQ and purchase order so they are planned and priced from the start.

## Store it properly

Keep documentation with equipment records in your maintenance system. When a component is replaced or repaired, the previous documentation is valuable reference.

## A supplier who documents is a supplier who controls

Suppliers who readily provide inspection and test records usually have controlled processes. Reluctance to provide documentation is a warning sign.
