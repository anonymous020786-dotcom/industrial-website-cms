Hydraulic oil is often treated as a consumable that simply needs topping up. In reality it is a working component of the system. It transmits power, lubricates moving parts, removes heat, protects against corrosion and carries contaminants to the filters. The wrong oil can shorten component life even when everything else is done right.

## What hydraulic oil has to do

- **Transmit power** with minimal compressibility.
- **Lubricate** pumps, motors, valves and seals.
- **Cool** the system by carrying heat to the reservoir and cooler.
- **Seal** small internal clearances.
- **Protect** against rust, corrosion and oxidation.
- **Release air** and separate from water quickly.

## Viscosity: the most important property

Viscosity is a fluid's resistance to flow. Hydraulic oils are classified by ISO viscosity grade (ISO VG), such as VG 32, VG 46 or VG 68. The number refers to viscosity at 40 °C.

- **Too thin:** internal leakage increases, lubricating films break down and wear accelerates.
- **Too thick:** flow resistance increases, pumps can cavitate on cold starts, and energy is wasted as heat.

Pump manufacturers specify an acceptable viscosity range for operation and for cold starting. The goal is to keep the oil within that range across the whole operating temperature range.

## Viscosity index

Viscosity changes with temperature. The **viscosity index (VI)** describes how much. A high-VI oil changes less between cold and hot conditions. High-VI oils are useful for equipment that works outdoors across wide temperature swings, such as mobile cranes and construction machinery.

## Common hydraulic fluid types

| Fluid type | Typical use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Anti-wear (AW) mineral oil | Most industrial and mobile systems | Contains anti-wear additives for pumps |
| High-VI mineral oil | Outdoor and mobile equipment | Stable viscosity over wide temperatures |
| Fire-resistant fluids | Steel plants, foundries, mining | Different types with different seal compatibility |
| Biodegradable fluids | Forestry, marine, environmentally sensitive sites | Check compatibility and maintenance needs |

## Additives

Hydraulic oils contain additive packages designed for specific duties:

- **Anti-wear additives** protect pump surfaces under high load.
- **Antioxidants** slow oil degradation at high temperatures.
- **Rust and corrosion inhibitors** protect metal surfaces.
- **Anti-foam agents** help release entrained air.
- **Demulsifiers** help water separate from the oil.

Mixing oils with different additive chemistries can cause additive reactions, sludge and filter blocking. Avoid mixing brands or types unless the supplier confirms compatibility.

## Matching oil to operating temperature

Consider three temperatures:

1. **Cold start temperature:** the oil must be thin enough for the pump to draw it without cavitation.
2. **Normal operating temperature:** the oil should stay within the optimum viscosity range.
3. **Maximum temperature:** the oil must not become too thin, and high temperatures accelerate oxidation.

If the system regularly runs hot, a heavier grade is not always the answer. The better solution is often to find the source of the heat and improve cooling.

## Fire-resistant and special fluids

Where hydraulic leaks could reach ignition sources — foundries, steel mills, die casting and underground mining — fire-resistant fluids may be required. These fluids can require different seals, hoses, filters and maintenance practices. Changing fluid type usually needs a system review and thorough flushing.

## Good oil management practices

- Use the grade specified by the equipment or pump manufacturer.
- Label containers and top-up points clearly to prevent mixing.
- Filter new oil before it enters the system.
- Monitor condition through oil analysis.
- Change oil based on condition, not just time.
- Investigate any unusual colour, smell, foaming or cloudiness.

## Summary

Choose oil primarily on viscosity for your temperature range, then select the right fluid type and additive package for the application. Keep it clean, dry and within temperature limits, and it will protect every component in the system.

Hot hydraulic oil is a warning sign. High temperatures thin the oil, increase internal leakage, harden seals and accelerate oil degradation. Running hot for long periods significantly shortens the life of pumps, seals and the oil itself.

The key to fixing overheating is understanding where the heat comes from.

## Where does the heat come from?

Hydraulic heat is wasted energy. Any time oil flows from high pressure to low pressure **without doing useful work**, that energy is converted into heat. Common sources include:

- oil flowing over the **relief valve**;
- flow being **throttled** through flow control valves;
- **internal leakage** in worn pumps, motors, valves and cylinders;
- **pressure drops** in undersized hoses, pipes and valves;
- a pump running at full flow when the machine is idle.

## Why heat is harmful

- **Viscosity drops**, increasing leakage and reducing lubricating film strength.
- **Seals harden**, crack and lose their sealing ability.
- **Oil oxidises** faster, forming varnish and sludge.
- **Efficiency falls**, so the system generates even more heat.

## Common causes and fixes

### 1. Relief valve set too low or system running over relief

If the relief valve opens during normal operation, all excess flow is converted to heat. Check the relief setting against the system requirements and check that other pressure settings, such as pump compensators, are correctly coordinated.

### 2. Pump running unloaded at full pressure

Fixed-displacement pumps in simple circuits may push full flow over the relief valve when actuators are idle. Options include unloading valves, open-centre valves that return flow to tank at low pressure, or variable-displacement pumps.

### 3. Worn components

Worn pumps and motors leak internally, converting energy to heat. Rising case drain flow on piston pumps and motors is a useful indicator of wear.

### 4. Undersized lines and valves

High flow velocities in small hoses and valves create pressure drops and heat. Review line sizes, particularly in return lines and after system modifications that increased flow.

### 5. Cooling problems

Air-blast coolers clog with dust and debris; water coolers scale up or lose flow. Fans fail, thermostats stick and coolers get bypassed. Clean and inspect coolers regularly.

### 6. Low oil level or undersized reservoir

A low oil level reduces the time oil spends in the tank and reduces heat dissipation. Systems that have grown over time may have outgrown their reservoir.

### 7. High ambient temperatures

Units in hot environments — near furnaces, in direct sun or in enclosed spaces — have less temperature difference available to shed heat. Ventilation, relocation or extra cooling may be needed.

## A practical troubleshooting sequence

1. **Measure** oil temperature at the reservoir and, if possible, across major components using an infrared thermometer.
2. **Check oil level** and cooler condition.
3. **Check relief and compensator settings** against specifications.
4. **Look for hot spots**: a valve or component much hotter than its surroundings is often where energy is being lost.
5. **Check case drain flows** on pumps and motors.
6. **Review the circuit** for throttling and unnecessary pressure drops.

## Design improvements

When overheating is caused by the design rather than a fault, consider:

- variable-displacement or load-sensing pumps;
- accumulators to meet peak demand with a smaller pump;
- larger coolers or adding cooling capacity;
- better reservoir design;
- reducing throttling losses with improved valve selection.

## Monitor temperature

Fit a temperature gauge or sensor on the reservoir and record readings. A gradual increase over months often indicates wear or cooler fouling, giving you time to act before failures occur.

A hydraulic accumulator is a pressure vessel that stores hydraulic fluid under pressure, usually against a compressed gas. When the system needs more flow than the pump can supply, the accumulator releases stored fluid. When demand drops, it refills.

Accumulators can make systems smaller, more efficient and smoother — but they also store energy that remains after the pump stops. Understanding how they work is essential for safe maintenance.

## How an accumulator works

Most industrial accumulators are **gas-charged**. A separating element — a bladder, piston or diaphragm — divides the vessel into a gas side, pre-charged with dry nitrogen, and an oil side connected to the hydraulic system.

As system pressure rises above the pre-charge pressure, oil enters and compresses the gas. When system pressure falls, the compressed gas pushes oil back into the circuit.

## Types of accumulators

| Type | Characteristics | Typical uses |
|---|---|---|
| Bladder | Fast response, good for pulsation damping and energy storage | Mobile and industrial systems |
| Piston | Large volumes, higher pressure ratios, tolerant of large shells | Heavy industry, large energy storage |
| Diaphragm | Compact, smaller volumes | Pulsation damping, small circuits |

## Common applications

- **Energy storage:** supply peak flow so a smaller pump and motor can be used.
- **Emergency power:** complete a movement, such as closing a valve or lowering a load safely, if power fails.
- **Pressure maintenance:** compensate for leakage and hold pressure for clamping without running the pump continuously.
- **Shock absorption:** absorb pressure spikes from fast valve shifts.
- **Pulsation damping:** smooth pump flow ripple.
- **Thermal expansion compensation:** absorb volume changes in closed circuits.

## The importance of pre-charge pressure

The nitrogen pre-charge pressure determines how the accumulator performs. If it is too low, the accumulator delivers less usable volume and the separating element can be damaged. If it is too high, oil may not enter at all.

Pre-charge pressure should be checked regularly with the correct charging and gauging equipment, with the oil side fully depressurised. Loss of pre-charge is a common, easily overlooked cause of poor system performance.

## Safety considerations

Accumulators store energy even when the pump is off. Many hydraulic injuries happen because people assume a stopped system is safe.

**Always:**

- **Discharge the oil side** of the accumulator before working on the circuit, using the system's dump or bleed valve, and confirm with a pressure gauge.
- **Use only nitrogen** for pre-charging. Never use oxygen or compressed air, which can cause explosions.
- Use proper **charging equipment** and follow the manufacturer's procedures.
- Respect the vessel's **pressure rating** and local pressure-vessel regulations, including any inspection or certification requirements.
- **Label** circuits containing accumulators so maintenance staff are aware of stored energy.
- Ensure accumulator circuits have a **safety block** with a relief valve and isolation and bleed valves.

## Signs of accumulator problems

- Pump cycling on and off more frequently than normal.
- Loss of emergency function or reduced cycle speed.
- Increased pressure spikes and noise.
- Pressure dropping quickly when the pump stops.

These often indicate lost pre-charge, a failed bladder or piston seal, or an incorrectly sized accumulator.

## Sizing accumulators

Accumulator sizing depends on the volume of oil needed, the minimum and maximum working pressures, the pre-charge pressure and whether gas compression is fast (adiabatic) or slow (isothermal). Correct sizing calculations are important both for performance and safety.
