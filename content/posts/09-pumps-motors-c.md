Many hydraulic systems waste a large share of their input power as heat. A common reason is the pump: a fixed-displacement pump delivers the same flow on every revolution, whether the machine needs it or not. When the actuators are idle or moving slowly, the excess flow goes over a relief valve or through throttling valves, turning energy into heat.

Variable displacement pumps solve this by adjusting their output to match demand.

## How a variable displacement pump works

Most variable displacement pumps in industrial and mobile systems are **axial piston pumps** with a movable swash plate. The angle of the swash plate sets the piston stroke — and therefore the flow per revolution. A control system changes the angle in response to system conditions. Some vane pumps also offer variable displacement by shifting the cam ring.

## Common control types

### Pressure compensation

The pump delivers full flow until system pressure reaches a set level. It then reduces displacement to deliver only enough flow to maintain that pressure. When actuators stop, the pump "destrokes" to almost zero flow while holding pressure. This avoids the heat that would be generated by dumping full flow over a relief valve.

### Load sensing

A load-sensing pump monitors the pressure required by the actuator and supplies just enough pressure and flow to meet it, plus a small margin. Load sensing is common in mobile hydraulics such as cranes and excavators, where many functions operate at different pressures and speeds.

### Power (horsepower) limiting

The pump limits its output so that pressure × flow does not exceed the available input power. This allows a smaller engine or motor to be used without stalling.

### Electronic and proportional control

Electronically controlled pumps adjust displacement in response to signals from a controller, allowing sophisticated energy-saving strategies and precise control.

## Where the savings come from

| Situation | Fixed pump | Variable pump |
|---|---|---|
| Actuators idle, pressure held | Full flow over relief → high heat | Near-zero flow, minimal loss |
| Slow movement needed | Excess flow throttled → heat | Flow reduced to demand |
| Low-pressure function | Pump works against higher pressure | Pressure matched to load (load sensing) |

The result is lower electricity or fuel consumption, cooler oil, longer component life and sometimes a smaller cooler and reservoir.

## When is a variable pump worth it?

A variable displacement pump costs more than a gear pump. The investment is usually justified when:

- the system spends a lot of time **holding pressure** with little flow;
- **demand varies widely** through the cycle;
- the system runs for **long hours** each day;
- **overheating** is a recurring problem;
- the power supply or engine size is **limited**.

For a simple machine that runs occasionally at constant demand, a fixed pump may remain the most economical choice.

## Other ways to improve efficiency

- **Accumulators** supply peak flow so a smaller pump can run at a steadier load.
- **Variable speed drives** on electric motors adjust pump speed to demand, an increasingly popular option for fixed and variable pumps alike.
- **Unloading circuits** send fixed-pump flow to tank at low pressure when idle.
- **Correct line sizing** reduces pressure losses.
- **Regular maintenance** prevents wear-related leakage and efficiency loss.

## Setting up variable pumps correctly

Variable pumps rely on correctly adjusted controls. Compensator and load-sensing settings must be coordinated with relief valve settings. Incorrect settings can cause instability, noise or excessive heat. Settings should be checked during commissioning and after maintenance.

## Retrofitting older systems

Older machines with fixed pumps can often be upgraded with a variable pump or a variable speed drive. The energy savings on high-utilisation machines can be significant, and the reduction in heat often improves reliability.

It is surprisingly common for a brand-new or freshly rebuilt hydraulic pump to fail within hours of installation. The cause is rarely a manufacturing defect. More often the pump was started dry, the system was dirty, or air was trapped inside. A careful start-up procedure costs a few extra minutes and protects a significant investment.

Always follow the pump manufacturer's specific instructions; the steps below summarise good general practice.

## Before installation

### 1. Find out why the old pump failed

If contamination, cavitation or overheating destroyed the previous pump, the new one will suffer the same fate unless the cause is corrected.

### 2. Clean the system

After a pump failure, debris spreads through the circuit. Flush the system, clean the reservoir, replace filter elements and check that hoses and valves are free of debris.

### 3. Check the specification

Confirm the replacement pump matches the original: displacement, rotation direction, shaft type, mounting flange, port sizes and controls.

## Installation

### 4. Align the drive

Check coupling alignment between the motor or engine and the pump. Misalignment overloads shaft bearings and seals. Use the correct coupling and do not force the pump onto the shaft.

### 5. Check the suction line

Make sure the suction line is clean, correctly sized, free of air leaks and that any isolation valve is **fully open**. Starting a pump with a closed suction valve can destroy it in seconds.

### 6. Connect the case drain correctly

On piston pumps and motors, the case drain line must run back to the tank with minimal restriction and terminate below the oil level. The highest drain port is usually used so the case stays full of oil.

## Filling and priming

### 7. Fill the pump case

For pumps with a case drain, fill the case with clean hydraulic oil through the uppermost drain port before starting. Running a piston pump with a dry case quickly damages bearings and sliding surfaces.

### 8. Fill the reservoir with filtered oil

Use the correct grade, filtered through a transfer cart.

### 9. Prime the suction side

Ensure the pump inlet is flooded with oil. Pumps mounted above the oil level may need priming according to the manufacturer's instructions.

## First start

### 10. Set controls to minimum

Set relief valves and compensators to low values where possible, and place directional valves in a position that allows flow back to tank at low pressure.

### 11. Check rotation

Briefly jog the motor to confirm the correct direction of rotation. Running a pump backwards can damage it quickly and will not produce flow.

### 12. Start and run at low pressure

Start the pump and run it unloaded for a few minutes. Watch for:

- flow returning to tank;
- unusual noise;
- leaks;
- rising temperature at the case.

### 13. Bleed air

Cycle actuators slowly through their full stroke several times at low pressure to purge air. Bleed points on cylinders and high points in the circuit help remove trapped air. Top up the reservoir as air is expelled.

## Bringing up to pressure

### 14. Gradually increase pressure

Increase relief and compensator settings in steps to the specified values, using calibrated gauges.

### 15. Check operation

Confirm actuator speeds, pressures and temperatures. Check case drain flow on piston pumps to establish a baseline for future monitoring.

### 16. Recheck after initial running

After the first hours of operation, check for leaks, re-check filter indicators (new pumps and cleaned systems can release debris), confirm oil level and temperature, and re-tighten fittings if required.

## Quick checklist

| Step | Done? |
|---|---|
| Root cause of previous failure corrected | ☐ |
| System flushed, filters replaced | ☐ |
| Correct pump, rotation and mounting | ☐ |
| Suction valve open, suction line sound | ☐ |
| Case filled with clean oil | ☐ |
| Rotation verified | ☐ |
| Started at low pressure, air bled | ☐ |
| Pressures set and verified | ☐ |
| Baseline readings recorded | ☐ |

Look at a piston pump or many hydraulic motors and you will see a small hose running back to the tank, separate from the main supply and return lines. That is the **case drain**. It is easy to overlook, but it plays an important role in protecting the unit — and it can tell you a lot about the unit's condition.

## What is case drain flow?

Hydraulic pumps and motors rely on small internal clearances lubricated by oil. Some oil inevitably leaks past pistons, slippers and valve plates into the housing (the case). This internal leakage lubricates and cools the rotating group.

The leaked oil must be returned to the reservoir. The case drain line provides that path at low pressure.

## Why the case drain must be unrestricted

The shaft seal of a pump or motor is designed for low case pressure. If the case drain is blocked, restricted or connected into a pressurised return line, case pressure rises. The results can include:

- **blown shaft seals** and external leakage;
- **damaged internal components** forced apart by case pressure;
- **overheating** because oil is not circulating through the case.

Common mistakes include using undersized drain hoses, connecting the drain into a common return line with high back pressure, routing the drain through a filter that becomes blocked, or fitting quick couplers that do not connect properly.

## Installation best practice

- Use the **correct hose size** recommended by the manufacturer.
- Route the drain **directly to the tank**, ending below the oil level.
- Use the **uppermost drain port** so the case remains full of oil.
- Avoid unnecessary fittings and restrictions.
- If a case drain filter is required, size it generously and monitor it.
- Fill the case with clean oil before first start.

## Case drain flow as a health indicator

As a pump or motor wears, internal clearances increase and more oil leaks into the case. **Rising case drain flow is one of the earliest and most reliable signs of wear** in piston units.

### How to measure it

With suitable flow meters or by timing flow into a measuring container under controlled conditions, case drain flow can be measured at a known pressure, speed and temperature. Measurements must be taken safely by trained personnel, following the manufacturer's guidance — case drain oil can be hot and under pressure if restricted.

### Interpreting results

Manufacturers often provide expected case drain values for new units. More useful in practice is **trending**: record a baseline when the unit is healthy and compare measurements over time under the same conditions.

| Trend | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Stable over time | Normal wear, unit healthy |
| Gradual increase | Progressive wear — plan repair |
| Sudden increase | Internal damage — investigate promptly |
| Very high flow with heat and noise | Imminent failure likely |

## Case drain and temperature

The case of a healthy pump runs slightly warmer than the reservoir. A case that runs much hotter than usual suggests increased internal leakage, poor case flushing or restricted drain flow.

## Do all hydraulic motors have case drains?

Not all. Gear motors and some orbital motors drain internally to the return port, while others have an optional external drain that becomes necessary when return line pressure is high — for example, when motors are connected in series. Always check the manufacturer's data sheet.

## Summary

- The case drain removes internal leakage and protects shaft seals.
- Never restrict or pressurise the case drain.
- Measuring and trending case drain flow is a simple, powerful condition-monitoring tool.
